When a police officer is finally dismissed or even convicted for gross misconduct, how often do their colleagues quietly nod and say something like, “I saw that coming”? More often than we’d like to admit, the signs of serious trouble are visible long before a scandal erupts. Yet in policing, we’ve traditionally focused on the big incidents and tended to wave off the small transgressions. This needs to change.

### Those Who Do Big Bad Things Also Do Little Bad Things

![](/images/image_editor_output_image1681454371-17655514280582700668508713128379.png)

Professor Jason Roach’s theory of self-selection policing offers a simple insight: “those who commit big bad things also commit little bad things”[1]. In other words, serious offenders often reveal themselves through minor offenses. By breaking even trivial rules, they are effectively “offering themselves up for police attention”[1]. Roach (with Professor Ken Pease) has spent years researching these “trigger offences” – like illegal parking in disabled bays or driving while disqualified – that frequently tip off police to bigger crimes[2][3].  
Self-selection policing is attractive because it is proactive and preventative. If catching criminals can start with a broken taillight or a minor infraction, could the same logic apply within police organisations? If an officer is willing to flout a small rule or behave badly in a “minor” way, might that be the warning sign – the self-selection trigger – that points to more serious misconduct down the line?

![](/images/share_62732086914530934196007772124391850255.png)

### Minor Misconduct as a Major Warning Sign

Instead of looking outward for offenders, let’s turn this lens inward. An officer who habitually bends the rules – whether it’s rudeness to the public, “tiny” dishonesty in paperwork, or ignoring protocols – may be signalling a deeper disregard for ethical boundaries. Many in policing can recall colleagues whose small behavioral issues were brushed aside for years, until one day they weren’t small at all.  
This is the crux of applying self-selection policing to professional standards: early low-level misconduct can be the red flag for later gross misconduct or even criminal activity by officers. It’s about recognizing that the officer who cuts corners or shows aggression in minor situations could be the same officer who causes serious harm down the road. Time and again when a major case hits – an excessive force incident, a corruption scandal – you hear internally that people weren’t surprised. The warning signs were there; they just weren’t acted upon.

### Dropping the Ball on Early Intervention

Right now, most Professional Standards Departments (PSD) don’t focus on the “small stuff.” They’re busy investigating serious misconduct and criminal allegations. So what happens to the lower-level misbehavior? Often it gets passed to line supervisors to deal with “informally.” A young constable with attitude problems, sloppy discipline, or minor misconduct might just get a talking-to from their sergeant – if that. The assumption is that if it’s not gross misconduct, it’s a “local matter.”  
The intention is to handle minor issues at the lowest level, but in practice this delegation to local supervisors is riddled with issues. Front-line supervisors are stretched thin with operational demands, and many have little training in spotting the bigger pattern behind a series of small incidents. As a result, crucial early warning signs get missed. Minor complaints or misconduct might be noted in isolation but not connected as a pattern of escalating behavior.  
Baroness Louise Casey’s 2023 review of the Metropolitan Police’s misconduct system[4] highlighted this problem. She found that behavior “which in most other organisations would lead to instant dismissal or serious disciplinary action” was often addressed through only light-touch measures like “reflective practices” in the Met[4]. Each incident was handled separately, with the force “failing to identify repeated or patterns of unacceptable behaviour” and potentially missing “escalating misconduct”[5]. In other words, the system was treating symptoms one by one and never diagnosing the disease.  
This isn’t just a Metropolitan Police issue. Research shows misconduct tends to be highly concentrated among a small number of officers. Studies have found that a small fraction of officers are responsible for a disproportionate share of misconduct[6] – the familiar “few bad apples” who account for much of the harm. One recent analysis of multiple U.S. police departments found about 5% of officers were responsible for around 20% of all citizen complaints[7], and a few officers generated an outsize number of use-of-force incidents and payouts for damages[8]. Criminologist Lawrence Sherman’s work on the Crime Harm Index and the “power few” in crime is apt here: most crime is committed by a small fraction of all criminals[9], and likewise most police misconduct is caused by a small cadre of repeat offenders in uniform.

### “I Saw That Coming”: The Colleague Intuition

When an officer finally crosses a big red line, the least surprised people are often their coworkers. Many officers can think of someone who was known for skating on thin ice for years. Maybe it was the constable who always had an attitude problem and racked up minor complaints, or the detective who played fast and loose with rules. When such individuals are eventually dismissed or even arrested, it’s common to hear fellow officers mutter, “It was only a matter of time.”  
Consider some recent cases. Before Metropolitan Police officer Wayne Couzens kidnapped and murdered Sarah Everard, he had been reported for indecent exposure multiple times[10] with no action taken. In fact, just days before the murder, Couzens exposed himself to women at a McDonald’s drive-through; one victim later said that if police had acted then, “we could have saved Sarah.”[11][12] Another Met officer, David Carrick, was nicknamed “Bastard Dave” by colleagues and had a long history of complaints (including eight separate reports of abuse) before he was finally prosecuted as a serial rapist[13]. These extreme cases make headlines, but they reflect a pattern: major harm by officers is nearly always preceded by minor harm that went unaddressed.  
Even beyond the UK, the pattern holds. In the United States, a Minneapolis officer who fatally shot an unarmed woman in 2017 had numerous red flags early in his career – training officers had raised concerns about his fitness for duty long before the tragedy[14]. He had even pulled a gun on a motorist during a routine traffic stop, pointing it at the driver’s head for a minor violation just weeks prior to the shooting[15] – a glaring warning sign that was never properly addressed. These anecdotes underscore the same point: within police organisations, people often know who the ticking time bombs are, based on their early conduct.

### Changing the Culture: Take the Small Stuff Seriously

To truly police our own, we need a cultural shift. The message from leadership must be that integrity is non-negotiable in every action, no matter how minor. That means empowering officers of all ranks to call out and correct small violations and concerning behaviors, not as petty nitpicking, but as proactive harm reduction. As the saying goes, “nip it in the bud.” Early intervention shouldn’t be seen as punitive, but as preventative – a way to steer a potentially troubled officer back on track or remove them before they do serious damage.  
Practically, this involves a few changes. First, training and supporting supervisors to recognize patterns and intervene early is crucial. Front-line sergeants need the time and tools to be effective gatekeepers of standards. They should not hesitate to document and address even low-level issues – a discourtesy here, a lapse of judgment there – and to flag patterns upward to PSD. Agencies are increasingly using early intervention systems – data-driven tools that monitor problematic behavior – to flag officers who may benefit from immediate retraining, counseling, or extra supervision[16][17]. These systems only work if there is a will to act on the alerts.  
Second, Professional Standards units must not dismiss the “small stuff” as beneath their remit. They should treat repeated minor misconduct as seriously as one major incident, because in terms of harm potential, it is. A pattern of small infractions can reveal an officer who poses a big risk. PSDs could adopt a policy of reviewing any officer with, say, three or more minor complaints in a year – not to punish them for those complaints in isolation, but to assess if there’s a deeper problem developing and intervene accordingly.  
Finally, there’s a need for organizational courage and support for whistleblowing. Officers must feel able to say “this isn’t right” about a colleague’s behavior without fear of ostracism. We know that a code of silence often protects misconduct[18]. Leaders have to break that culture by rewarding integrity and backing those who speak up. If multiple team members quietly believe Officer X is trouble, that should prompt action – not collective shoulder-shrugging. A healthy organization is one where saying “I’m worried about Bob’s behavior” is seen as loyalty to the service’s values, not a betrayal of colleagues.

### Conclusion: Proactive Harm Reduction

In summary, the theory of self-selection policing tells us that people reveal their true selves in small ways before they ever commit headline-grabbing acts. Policing has started to embrace this approach externally – catching big criminals through little crimes. Now it’s time to fully embrace it internally. Every police force has its share of low-level grumbles and “bad feeling” about certain individuals. Rather than dismissing that as gossip or personality clashes, we should pay attention. When patterns emerge – even of minor rule-breaking or discourtesy – they should trigger a closer look. It’s not about punishing every small mistake; it’s about recognizing the smoke before there’s a fire.  
The stakes are high. A single officer who goes rogue can inflict harm far disproportionate to their number – undermining public trust, harming victims, and tarnishing the reputation of thousands of honest officers. But rarely does that fall from grace happen overnight; the seeds are sown in the early, smaller transgressions that we too often ignore. For police leaders and policymakers, the challenge is clear: build systems and a culture that catch the small things and connect the dots. As one victim’s testimony made heartbreakingly clear, if we hold people accountable when warning signs first appear, we can prevent the worst outcomes. In policing, no act of misconduct is ever truly “small” – it’s an opportunity to intervene, to redirect, or to remove, before it’s too late.
