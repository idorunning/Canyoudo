Every council ward in England and Wales now has a named, contactable police officer. The government hit its first milestone — **3,000** additional neighbourhood personnel — in January 2026, ahead of schedule, on the way to **13,000** officers, PCSOs and special constables in neighbourhood roles by 2029. The January 2026 White Paper put the Neighbourhood Policing Guarantee at the centre of the most significant reshaping of policing in a generation.[^1]

The Guarantee is built on the language of evidence: presence "guided by the data," hotspot patrolling, visible neighbourhood policing. That language is welcome. But it carries two different jobs under one banner, and they are not the same job. A named officer for every ward is a confidence intervention. Hotspot patrolling is a crime-reduction intervention. The evidence for each is strong, and it is specific. The design honours that evidence in some places and quietly walks away from it in others.

A note explains the figure covers officers, PCSOs and special constables spread across every ward in England and Wales.](/images/neighbourhood-guarantee-chart1-milestone.png)
*The Guarantee by the numbers. The first tranche arrived early; the bulk is still to come, and how it is deployed will decide what it delivers.*

## What the hotspot evidence actually says

Hotspot policing is one of the best-supported tactics in the field, and it is worth being precise about why, because the precision is the whole point.

Crime is not spread evenly. A consistent finding across decades and cities is that around half of all crime concentrates in a tiny fraction of places — a few per cent of street segments and addresses.[^2] Put officers in those places and crime falls there, and the effect does not simply move around the corner. That much is settled.

What is less often carried across into policy is the *dosage*. Christopher Koper's 1995 study of patrol in Minneapolis hotspots found that the benefit of a patrol visit follows a curve. A visit of around **15 minutes** bought the most deterrence: after the officer left, the spot stayed quiet for a stretch — residual deterrence — far longer than the visit itself. Stay much longer than 15 minutes and the extra time bought little; the officer would have done more good moving to the next hotspot.[^3] [Lawrence Sherman](/influential-people/lawrence-sherman-and-the-invention-of-evidence-based-policing) and David Weisburd's randomised trial in the same city showed the headline result: concentrate patrol in the right small places and crime and disorder drop, measurably and significantly.[^4]

![A line chart of the Koper curve. The crime-free time that follows a patrol visit rises with the length of the visit, peaks at around 15 minutes, then declines. Short ambient presence of a few minutes does little; parking an officer at the spot all shift wastes them.](/images/neighbourhood-guarantee-chart2-koper-curve.png)
*Dosage is the active ingredient. Short, sharp, repeated visits buy deterrence. Thin everyday presence buys little, and so does parking an officer there all day. Illustrative, after Koper and Sherman & Weisburd.*

The active ingredient, in other words, is not presence in general. It is concentrated presence, in tight places, in short repeated bursts, with the dose watched. Recent work has refined the picture further — more frequent, shorter visits may beat fewer long ones — but every version of the finding turns on dosage discipline.[^5] Dilute the dose and you do not get a smaller version of the effect. You get no effect.

## Where the design and the evidence part company

Here is the tension. The White Paper commits forces to making hotspot patrolling a mainstream, everyday part of neighbourhood policing, with presence directed by the data.[^6] "Mainstream" and "everyday" are reassuring words. They are also close to the opposite of what the hotspot evidence rests on, which is targeted, time-limited, carefully dosed bursts.

The risk is concrete and it has a predictable shape. A proven tactic gets folded into business as usual. "Hotspot patrolling" comes to mean officers passing through busy areas when they can, for as long as they happen to have, rather than 15 focused minutes in the few street segments the data has flagged. The dose dissolves into ambient presence. The crime effect, which depended on the dose, does not appear. And the conclusion drawn is the wrong one: that hotspots "don't work here" — when what was tested was never hotspot patrolling in the sense the evidence means.

That would be a costly mistake to bank, because the tactic does work when it is done as designed. The failure would be one of implementation dressed up as a failure of the evidence.

## The confidence job is real, and it is a different job

None of this means the rest of the Guarantee is misconceived. A named, contactable officer for every ward is a reasonable thing to want, and the evidence supports it — for confidence.

The College of Policing's 2025 evidence guide on public confidence is clear that neighbourhood policing lifts public perceptions when three things run together: a targeted visible presence, community engagement that surfaces what local people are actually worried about, and problem-solving that does something about it.[^7] Confidence in the police fell to record lows through the austerity years and a run of high-profile scandals, and visible, familiar, contactable neighbourhood officers are a sound response to that. The Guarantee is well-built for this aim.

But improving how safe people feel and how much they trust the police is not the same outcome as cutting crime, and the two are measured differently and move differently. A ward officer who is known, reachable and responsive can raise confidence without necessarily shifting the burglary count — and concentrated hotspot bursts can cut the burglary count in places where few people will ever notice the patrol. Spreading 13,000 bodies thinly so that every one of England and Wales's **8,000-plus** wards has its named officer is a sound confidence model. It is not the dosage model the crime-reduction evidence is built on. Thirteen thousand headcount — including PCSOs and specials, not full-time patrol officers, spread across every ward and every shift — leaves a fraction of an officer in any given place at any given time. That is enough to be known. It is not enough to be a dose.[^1]

## Why this matters for the people delivering it

Leaders are being asked to deliver the Guarantee while being measured against a new national performance framework.[^6] That makes the distinction between the two jobs more than academic. If a named-officer scheme, which can credibly move confidence, is judged against crime figures it was never dosed to move, it will look like a failure. If diluted "hotspot" presence is logged as the proven tactic and then assessed on crime, it will underdeliver and take the reputation of hotspot policing down with it. Knowing which outcome each element of the design can actually shift is what lets a chief defend their resourcing choices and avoid promising a confidence measure a crime result it cannot give.

The sensible design choice is to keep the two clearly apart: run the named-officer Guarantee as the confidence programme it is good at, and run hotspot patrolling as a separate, dosed, data-targeted crime programme with the discipline the evidence demands — short bursts, tight places, the dose watched — rather than letting the second dissolve into the first.

## The verdict

So, does 13,000 spread thin beat fewer officers deployed with dosage discipline? For confidence, the thin spread is the point: presence everywhere is what makes officers known and reachable. For crime, it is the wrong instrument, and no amount of it substitutes for getting the dose right in the few places that carry most of the harm.

The Guarantee is well-designed as a confidence intervention and under-specified as a crime-reduction one. The mistake to avoid is not the policy. It is the scoreboard: reporting a confidence programme and a crime programme against the same yardstick, and being surprised when one of them appears to fail. The evidence for each is strong. It is strong about different things, and the design should say so.
