For most of its history, British policing ran on craft and instinct. What worked was what the experienced officers in the room said worked, and the test of a tactic was whether it felt right, not whether anyone had measured it. Alex Murray spent his career arguing that this was not good enough — and, unusually for a reformer, he did it from the inside, in uniform, while rising through the ranks.

He is the closest thing evidence-based policing in Britain has to a founder.

### The Society that started in a room

In 2010, Murray and a small group of fellow officers set up the Society of Evidence Based Policing (SEBP). It was, by its own account, the first body of its kind anywhere in the world: a network run by practitioners rather than academics, with the simple aim of helping officers use, produce and share the best available research on what actually reduces crime and harm.[1][2] Murray served as its founding chair from 2010 to 2014, and the Society he built is still going — the organisation behind much of the "what works" conversation in UK policing today.[1]

The early SEBP did something clever. Rather than lecture the service from the outside, it recruited respected names to lend it weight. It elected the criminologist **Lawrence Sherman** — the Cambridge professor whose work gave evidence-based policing much of its intellectual foundation — as its honorary president, alongside the then chief constable of Greater Manchester, Sir Peter Fahy.[2] That pairing of a leading academic with a serving chief was the model in miniature: research and operational policing in the same room, taken equally seriously.

### Why "evidence-based" is not as obvious as it sounds

The phrase can sound like a truism. Of course policing should be based on evidence. Who would argue for the opposite?

In practice, it cuts against a great deal of how policing has always worked. Evidence-based policing asks forces to test their tactics — ideally through randomised trials, the same method used to test medicines — and to be willing to drop the ones that do not work, however popular or long-established. It asks officers to target the small number of people, places and times that produce most harm, rather than spreading effort evenly. And it asks leaders to treat "we have always done it this way" as a reason for suspicion rather than comfort. Sherman's research on hot spots and on the concentration of harm gave the movement its evidence; people like Murray gave it a foothold inside the organisation.

### A practitioner, not a commentator

What makes Murray influential is that he never left operational policing to become a full-time advocate. He came up through the West Midlands, serving in every rank below chief officer across counter-terrorism, neighbourhood policing and area command.[2] He took the Cambridge route that Sherman helped build — a Master's in applied criminology — and in 2008 wrote a thesis on police legitimacy in Muslim communities, later published with Sherman and Katrin Mueller-Johnson.[3] He went on to join the Metropolitan Police as a Commander in 2020, holding one of the more demanding serious-crime portfolios in the country, and has been the national police lead for artificial intelligence.[1] He has also advised on police training abroad, in India, Cambodia and across Europe.[2]

The recognition followed the work. In 2013 the American academy that tracks the field elected him to its Evidence-Based Policing Hall of Fame, and in 2017 he was appointed OBE, in part for founding SEBP and for his contribution to evidence-based policing.[2][4]

### The 24-hour conference

The flavour of Murray's approach is captured by one of SEBP's stunts. The Society once ran a global, 24-hour evidence-based policing conference — a relay of presentations handed around the world through every time zone, so that a practitioner in Australia, Britain or North America could always find a session running.[4] It was part serious, part theatrical, and entirely the point: making research feel like something officers do, together, rather than something done to them by universities.

That instinct — meet officers where they are, make the evidence usable, keep it practical — runs through everything he has built. The criticism sometimes levelled at evidence-based policing is that it is academic, slow, and detached from the realities of a Friday night. Murray's contribution was to show that it need not be any of those things.

### Why he belongs on this list

Movements need someone willing to do the unglamorous organising: to start the society, chair the meetings, recruit the names, and keep going when the idea is still fringe. In British policing, that person was Alex Murray. The reason a serving inspector can today cite a randomised trial in a tactical meeting, or a force can stand up a "what works" function without it seeming eccentric, owes a great deal to the institution he founded in 2010 and the example he set by doing it while still chasing the work himself.

The next figure worth understanding is the academic on the other side of that founding partnership — Lawrence Sherman — whose research gave the movement its method. But the movement needed a police officer to carry it through the door. That was Murray's role, and he played it better than anyone.
