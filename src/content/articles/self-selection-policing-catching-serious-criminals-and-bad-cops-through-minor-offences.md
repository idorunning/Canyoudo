**The most useful fact in crime detection may be this: people who do big bad things also do little bad things.** A burglar does not stop being a rule-breaker when he gets out of the car, and a serious offender rarely confines his offending to the serious. He parks where he should not, drives when he is banned, skips the insurance, jumps the fare — and on his days off, he shoplifts. Each of those minor offences is trivial in itself, and each one is a small flag, raised by the offender himself, saying _look at me_. Self-selection policing is the discipline of noticing the flag.

The approach was developed and championed by [Professor Jason Roach](/influential-people/jason-roach-and-the-art-of-catching-criminals-by-their-small-mistakes) and Professor Ken Pease, who set it out in full in their book [_Self-Selection Policing: Theory, Research and Practice_](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1057/978-1-137-46852-9) (Palgrave Macmillan, 2016). Their phrase for the underlying premise has become the field's shorthand: ["those who do big bad things also do little bad things"](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/stopping-crime/202211/the-self-selection-policing-approach). The job of self-selection policing (SSP) is to identify which "little bad things" are the most reliable signposts to the big ones — and then make sure officers treat those minor offences as an opportunity rather than a nuisance.

### What it actually means

Most proactive policing works from the person to the offence: intelligence suggests someone is an active offender, so police watch them and wait for the crime. Self-selection policing reverses the direction. It works from the offence to the person: a minor offence is committed in plain sight, and the offender — by committing it — _selects himself_ for a second look. Nobody had to profile him. Nobody had to guess. As Roach and Pease put it, by committing small offences, active serious criminals are ["offering themselves up for police attention"](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/stopping-crime/202211/the-self-selection-policing-approach).

That reversal matters for two reasons. First, it is **proactive without being speculative** — the trigger is an actual offence, dealt with on its own terms, not a hunch about a person. Second, it is **cheap**. The encounter is already happening: the parking ticket is already being written, the disqualified driver is already stopped, the shoplifter is already detained. SSP simply asks the officer to spend two more minutes asking who they are dealing with.

### Burglars shoplift on their days off

Someone said to me once that "burglars shoplift on their days off." The line stuck, because it captures the whole theory in five words. Offenders are not specialists working a single trade; the disposition that makes a person break into a house is the same disposition that makes them pocket a bottle of spirits from a supermarket, drive while banned, or leave the car across a disabled bay. Crime is not compartmentalised, and neither are criminals.

The practical pay-off is striking once you turn it around. If the people committing the most serious acquisitive crime are also, routinely, the people committing the most ordinary shop theft, then **the shoplifter in front of you is statistically far more likely than a random member of the public to be a burglar, a drug dealer, or someone wanted on a live warrant.** By taking shop theft seriously — by checking who the shoplifter actually is, rather than processing the offence and moving on — a force is not only addressing retail crime. It is fishing in a pool richly stocked with exactly the active, prolific offenders it most wants to find. By catching shoplifters, in other words, you catch burglars.

That is precisely the wrong lesson to draw from the long retreat from "low-value" shoplifting enforcement. A shop theft written off as not worth the paperwork is not just an unaddressed retail loss; it is a self-selected serious offender, identified in plain sight, waved back out of the door.

### The evidence: disabled bays and disqualified drivers

The founding study is almost charmingly mundane. In Huddersfield in the late 1990s, [Sylvia Chenery, Chris Henshaw and Ken Pease examined cars parked illegally in disabled bays](https://popcenter.asu.edu/sites/default/files/problems/bicycle_theft/PDFs/Chenery_etal_1999.pdf) — published by the Home Office in 1999 as _Illegal Parking in Disabled Bays: A Means of Offender Targeting_. The hypothesis was that the most committed criminals are also the most versatile, unwilling to be bound by law or convention of any kind, right down to where they leave the car. The results bore it out: [around one in four of the vehicles](https://news-archive.hud.ac.uk/news/2015/may/helpingthepolicewiththeirenquiriesresearchaidspolicing.php) parked illicitly in disabled bays was of immediate police interest — registered to people with live warrants, disqualifications or active criminal involvement, at rates far beyond anything a random sample of motorists would produce.

Driving while disqualified turns out to be an even stronger signal. [Roach's study of disqualified drivers](https://academic.oup.com/policing/article-abstract/13/3/300/2951290), published in _Policing: A Journal of Policy and Practice_ (13(3)), examined the offending histories of a sample of drivers caught behind the wheel while banned — and found an overwhelming proportion had convictions for other, often serious, offences, with offending patterns closer to mainstream career criminals than to ordinary motoring offenders. The logic is intuitive once stated: a person legally banned from driving who drives anyway is demonstrating, in real time, that court orders do not constrain them.

The wider literature points the same way. Studies in [the UK, the USA, Australia, New Zealand and parts of Europe](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/310517679_Self-Selection_Policing) have repeatedly found links between serious driving offences — drink-driving, dangerous driving, driving while disqualified — and involvement in mainstream violent and acquisitive crime. The "trigger offences" are not arbitrary: they are the minor offences whose commission statistically enriches the pool of people worth a closer look.

### Why it's useful

For a service permanently short of resource, SSP has a rare combination of virtues:

- **It finds active offenders, now.** Conviction records tell you who offended in the past. A trigger offence is being committed in the present, by someone currently willing to break rules — exactly the population current policing most wants to find.
- **It piggybacks on routine work.** No new patrols, no surveillance budget. The contact is already happening; SSP changes what the officer does with it. The marginal cost is curiosity.
- **It is fair in a way profiling is not.** The trigger is conduct, not character. Nobody is checked because of who they are, what they look like, or where they live — only because of an offence they chose to commit, in public, in front of an officer. The offender genuinely does select himself.
- **It complements the rest of the evidence-based toolkit.** [Hot-spots policing](/topics/hotspot-policing) tells you _where_ to be; self-selection tells you _who_, among the people you meet there, deserves the second question. It slots naturally into the targeting–testing–tracking framework this site has described in its [profile of Lawrence Sherman](/influential-people/lawrence-sherman-and-the-invention-of-evidence-based-policing).

### Turning the lens inward: catching the bad cops

Here is the part that makes serving officers uncomfortable, and it should. The insight at the heart of self-selection policing is about rule-breakers — and it does not stop applying when the rule-breaker wears a uniform.

When a police officer is finally dismissed, or even convicted, for gross misconduct, it is striking how often colleagues quietly nod and say something like, "I saw that coming." More often than we would like to admit, the signs of serious trouble are visible long before a scandal erupts. Yet in policing we have traditionally focused on the big incidents and waved off the small transgressions. If catching criminals can begin with a parking violation or a fare dodge, the same logic may apply within police organisations. An officer who is willing to flout a small rule, or behave badly in a minor way, may be displaying the warning sign — the self-selection trigger — that points to more serious misconduct later on.

#### Minor misconduct as a major warning sign

An officer who habitually bends the rules — through rudeness to the public, small dishonesty in paperwork, or ignoring protocols — may be signalling a deeper disregard for ethical boundaries. Many in policing can recall colleagues whose small behavioural issues were brushed aside for years, until one day they were no longer small at all. This is the heart of applying self-selection policing to professional standards: early low-level misconduct can be the red flag for later gross misconduct, or even criminal activity, by officers. The officer who cuts corners or shows aggression in minor situations may be the same officer who causes serious harm later.

#### The missed chance for early intervention

At present, most Professional Standards Departments (PSDs) do not focus on the small stuff. They are occupied with investigating serious misconduct and criminal allegations; lower-level misbehaviour is often passed to line supervisors to deal with informally. The intention — handle minor issues at the lowest level — is sensible, but in practice this delegation carries real problems. Front-line supervisors are stretched thin, and many have little training in spotting the larger pattern behind a series of small incidents. Crucial early warning signs are noted in isolation but never connected.

Baroness Louise Casey's 2023 review of the Metropolitan Police's misconduct system found exactly this: behaviour ["which in most other organisations would lead to instant dismissal or serious disciplinary action"](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/casey-review-misconduct-in-the-met-and-officer-dismissal/) was often addressed through light-touch measures such as "reflective practices," with the force failing to identify repeated patterns of unacceptable behaviour and so missing escalating misconduct. The system was treating symptoms one by one and never diagnosing the disease.

This is not solely a Met issue. Research shows misconduct tends to be highly concentrated among a small number of officers — the familiar "few bad apples." One analysis of multiple U.S. police departments found about [5% of officers were responsible for around 20% of all citizen complaints](https://nnscommunities.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/Police-Misconduct-Issue-Brief-Addressing-the-Few.pdf), with a few officers generating an outsize share of use-of-force incidents and damages payouts. Criminologist Lawrence Sherman's work on the [Crime Harm Index and the "power few"](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2007/11/071119113913.htm) is apt: most crime is committed by a small fraction of all criminals, and likewise most police misconduct is caused by a small cadre of repeat offenders in uniform. Find that cadre early and you prevent a disproportionate share of the harm.

#### "I saw that coming"

Consider some recent cases. Before Metropolitan Police officer Wayne Couzens kidnapped and murdered Sarah Everard, he had been [reported for indecent exposure multiple times](https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2024/feb/29/police-could-and-should-have-stopped-him-key-points-wayne-couzens-report) with no action taken; just days before the murder he exposed himself at a McDonald's drive-through, and one victim later said that if police had acted then, ["we could have saved Sarah."](https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2023/may/02/police-consider-rethink-on-indecent-exposure-after-wayne-couzens-scandal) Another Met officer, David Carrick, was nicknamed "Bastard Dave" by colleagues and had [a long history of complaints](https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2024/sep/30/four-officers-face-disciplinary-action-over-david-carrick-investigation), including multiple reports of abuse, before he was finally prosecuted as a serial rapist. These extreme cases make headlines, but they reflect a pattern: major harm by officers is nearly always preceded by minor harm that went unaddressed.

The pattern holds beyond the UK. In the United States, a Minneapolis officer who fatally shot an unarmed woman in 2017 had [numerous red flags early in his career](https://www.foxnews.com/us/records-cop-accused-in-australians-death-cant-take-stress) — training officers had raised concerns about his fitness for duty, and he had pointed a gun at a motorist's head during a routine stop just weeks before the shooting. Within police organisations, people often know who the likely future offenders are, based on their early conduct. The intelligence is already in the building.

#### Taking the small stuff seriously

To police our own effectively, the message from leadership must be that integrity is non-negotiable in every action, however minor — and that early intervention is preventative, not punitive. In practice that means three things. First, training and supporting supervisors to recognise patterns and intervene early, with the time and tools to be genuine gatekeepers of standards. Agencies are increasingly using [early intervention systems](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/article/police-misconduct-peer-effects/) — data-driven tools that flag officers who may benefit from retraining, counselling or extra supervision — but these only work where there is a will to act on the alerts. Second, PSDs treating repeated minor misconduct as seriously as a single major incident: a force might review any officer with, say, three or more minor complaints in a year, not to punish the complaints in isolation but to assess whether a deeper problem is developing. Third, organisational courage on whistleblowing, breaking the [code of silence](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/article/police-misconduct-peer-effects/) so that saying "I'm worried about this colleague" is treated as loyalty to the service's values, not betrayal of a workmate.

The symmetry is the point. Whether the rule-breaker is a burglar shoplifting on his day off or an officer cutting corners on the small stuff, the signal works the same way — and a service that learns to read it can act before the small thing becomes a large one.

### The honest limits

An evidence-based explainer owes you the caveats, and there are real ones.

**Base rates still rule.** "One in four of police interest" means three in four are not. A trigger offence justifies a check, never a presumption; the follow-up must be proportionate to what the check actually finds. Treating every disabled-bay parker — or every officer with a brusque manner — as a master criminal would be both wrong and counterproductive.

**Triggers must be chosen by evidence, not intuition.** The whole approach collapses into ordinary prejudice the moment "offences that statistically flag serious offenders" drifts into "behaviour that annoys officers". Roach and Pease are explicit that candidate triggers need empirical validation — which minor offences, tested against records, actually enrich for serious offending — and that some intuitively appealing candidates fail the test. The same discipline applies inside the organisation: the trigger is a pattern of genuine rule-breaking, not personality, politics or unpopularity.

**Implementation is harder than the idea.** [Roach's disqualified-driver work](https://academic.oup.com/policing/article-abstract/13/3/300/2951290) itself concludes that obstacles — data access at the roadside, officer time, recording practice — must be cleared before trigger offences can be systematically embedded in practice. Like most of evidence-based policing, the science is the easy half; the organisational plumbing is the hard half.

**Disproportionality needs watching.** Even conduct-based triggers can fall unevenly if enforcement of the minor offence itself is uneven. A force adopting SSP should track who is being checked, exactly as it should for [stop and search](/topics/stop-and-search).

### What a force would actually do

The practical version is unglamorous, which is rather the appeal. Pick two or three empirically validated trigger offences — driving while disqualified is the best-evidenced candidate, and shop theft is a strong, under-used one. Brief officers that these offences warrant a routine extra check: who is this person, what is attached to them, does anything live come back. Record the outcomes, so the force can test — in the proper, [evidence-based](/topics/evidence-based-policing) sense — whether its triggers are earning their keep, and retire the ones that are not. Then turn the same lens inward: give PSDs and supervisors a way to spot and act on patterns of minor misconduct before they escalate. That last step — testing the hypothesis — is what separates self-selection policing from a slogan.

The deeper lesson generalises beyond any single tactic. Offenders are not specialists, crime is not compartmentalised, and the everyday infractions police see constantly — out on the street and inside the station — are not noise. They are data. The service that learns to read them gets something close to free intelligence, handed over voluntarily, by the very people it most needs to find.
