*This piece updates my earlier analysis of the Somerton Man code. The first version proposed a single base location and a rail-led reading of the letters. What follows widens the transport assumptions to a multimodal model, sets out the search interpretation as the organising idea, tests and rejects the suggestion that Webb's estranged wife explains his presence in South Australia, and then does the thing my first draft did not do properly: it tries to break its own hypothesis. The analysis tools behind it are now public too — see the work-in-progress [Somerton Lab](/somerton-lab/).*

## A death that refuses to file itself away

On the morning of 1 December 1948, two men exercising racehorses found a body on Somerton Park beach, opposite the Crippled Children's Home on the corner of The Esplanade and Bickford Terrace. The man was well dressed, lying back against the seawall with his legs extended and his feet crossed. A half-smoked cigarette rested on the right collar of his coat. He had been seen the previous evening in the same spot by witnesses who assumed he was asleep or drunk.

He carried no wallet and no identity documents. The labels had been cut from his clothing. An unused second-class rail ticket to Henley Beach sat in his pocket alongside a used bus ticket toward Glenelg, a packet of Juicy Fruit, an aluminium comb of American manufacture, and an Army Club cigarette packet holding seven cigarettes of a more expensive brand, Kensitas.

That is the kind of opening that makes a case famous. What kept it famous was everything that failed to resolve afterwards: the enlarged spleen and organ congestion that pointed at poison no test could find, the rolled "Tamám Shud" slip torn from a copy of the *Rubáiyát of Omar Khayyám*, the book that turned up in a Glenelg car with a local nurse's phone number in the back, and five lines of pencilled capital letters that no cryptographer has cracked.

</figure>

I am a serving police inspector. I read this case the way I read any cold job: I separate what is established from what is inferred, I keep my own pet theory on a short lead, and I ask what evidence would prove me wrong. This article is about one hypothesis, stated as plainly as I can manage, together with an account of why it might be nonsense.

## What 2022 actually settled, and what it did not

For seventy-four years the central fact of the case was an absence: nobody knew who the man was. That changed in July 2022, when Professor Derek Abbott of the University of Adelaide and the American forensic genealogist Colleen Fitzpatrick announced that the Somerton Man was Carl "Charles" Webb, an electrical engineer and instrument maker born in Footscray, Melbourne, on 16 November 1905.

The identification rested on DNA recovered from hairs embedded in the plaster death mask. Earlier University of Adelaide work, at the Australian Centre for Ancient DNA in 2017–18, had recovered only mitochondrial DNA, placing the man in haplogroup H4a1a1a, which is carried by about 1 per cent of Europeans and narrows a maternal line but cannot name a person. The breakthrough came from a single rootless hair shaft analysed by a Californian laboratory, which yielded enough genome-wide data to drive a genealogical search. From a family tree of roughly 4,000 people, built out from a distant cousin match, Webb emerged as the candidate with the right profile. Abbott described the process to *The Age* as "like a sudoku puzzle with 4,000 elements," adding that "basically, there were just two people in there with no dates of death." The decisive connection was a first cousin three times removed on Webb's mother's side. A match to living relatives on 23 July 2022 closed the loop.

I treat Webb's identity as the settled foundation of everything that follows. I should be candid that this is not the same as official confirmation. The remains were exhumed on 19 May 2021 under South Australia Police's Operation Persevere, and Forensic Science SA has been working on the recovered material. As of the most recent reporting, neither South Australia Police nor the State Coroner has issued a formal identification; this is a coronial matter in which police submit a brief and the Coroner makes the legal determination, and that determination has not been delivered. The academic identification and the official process are two separate tracks. I am persuaded by the academic identification because the genealogical chain is coherent and independently documented, but a reader is entitled to hold it at "very probably" rather than "proven" until the Coroner speaks.

Two corrections to the popular story are worth making early, because they matter for the rest. The DNA work did not confirm the most romantic theory attached to the case. It demolished it. And the most widely repeated explanation for why Webb was in South Australia at all does not survive contact with the records.

## The man in the divorce file

The Webb who emerges from the archives is not a spy and not a glamorous figure. He is a sad one.

He was the youngest of six children in a family that ran a bakery. He trained as an electrical instrument maker, played club football, and by the records that survive lived a narrow and increasingly troubled life. On 4 October 1941 he married Dorothy Jean Robertson, a chiropodist; the couple lived in a flat in Bromby Street, South Yarra, and had no children.

The marriage failed badly. Dorothy's later divorce material describes a man who was solitary, moody, and at times violent and threatening, who kept few friends and went to bed early, and who was fond of poetry and wrote his own verse, "most of them on the subject of death, which he claims to be his greatest desire." She stated that in March 1946 he had attempted suicide with an overdose of ether. He deserted her around April 1947. In October 1951, three years after the Somerton Man's death, she placed a notice in the *Age* newspaper stating she had begun divorce proceedings against Webb on the ground of desertion; the divorce was granted in 1952.

There are two threads in that file that bear on why Webb might have been in South Australia, and the first needs correcting rather than repeating. After the 2022 identification, the widely reported explanation was that Webb had come to find his estranged wife: Dorothy later lived in Bute, a grain town roughly 144 kilometres north-northwest of Adelaide, and Abbott suggested the connection. "It's possible that he came to this state to try and find her," he told CNN. "This is just us drawing the dots."

The dots do not connect. The first document placing Dorothy in South Australia is her divorce affidavit, sworn at Kadina in June 1951, almost three years after Webb died. The 1949 Victorian electoral roll places her in Melbourne, at 69 Murphy Street, Elsternwick, still working as a chiropodist, and carries an annotation that she left that address in March 1950. No electoral roll, rate book, directory or local newspaper puts her in Bute, or anywhere in South Australia, in 1948. Abbott concedes the point himself: the only evidence of her in Bute is the 1951 document, and the Bute town history group holds no record of her. Nor does any source describe Webb looking for her. The search-for-Dorothy reading is inference, acknowledged as such by its author, stacked on a residence the records date to 1951. A man hunting his wife in Bute in 1948 would have been hunting a woman who was not there. I treat the Bute theory as closed unless a 1948 record surfaces, and the rest of this article proceeds without it.

The second thread is the name on the clothing. The suitcase recovered from the Adelaide Railway Station cloakroom held garments marked with variants of "T. Keane." Webb's brother-in-law was named Thomas Keane and lived a short drive away in Victoria, which offers a mundane explanation for marked clothing that police in 1948 treated as a deliberate clue. As Abbott put it, "it seems logical that he got his hand-me-downs from his brother-in-law."

Webb also, by the divorce record and Abbott's research, bet on horses. Hold that fact. It returns later as the strongest rival to everything I am about to argue.

## The paternity belief, stated plainly

For more than a decade the most compelling theory in the case was that the Somerton Man had fathered a child with Jessica Thomson, the Glenelg nurse whose phone number was found in the book.

Jessica Ellen Thomson, née Harkness, trained as a nurse at Royal North Shore Hospital in Sydney during the war. She had a documented habit of giving away copies of the *Rubáiyát*; the copy she gave to Lieutenant Alf Boxall in 1945, signed "JEstyn," survived intact and was produced when police tracked Boxall down alive in 1949, briefly demolishing their assumption that Boxall was the dead man. She moved to Adelaide, formed a relationship with Prosper Thomson, and her son Robin was born in July 1947, when she was unmarried and living at Moseley Street, Glenelg. Shown the plaster bust of the dead man in 1949, she was, by Detective Sergeant Lionel Leane's account and that of the cast's maker Paul Lawson, so affected she appeared about to faint. She maintained she did not know the man. Her daughter Kate later said her mother had admitted lying to police and knew more than she told.

Robin Thomson shared two rare traits with the Somerton Man: hypodontia, the congenital absence of certain teeth, and an ear in which the upper hollow is larger than the lower. The combined rarity of those traits became the backbone of the inherited-link theory, though the figures cited vary widely and should be treated with care: many popular accounts give the chance of coincidence as "one in 10 to 20 million," while the anatomist Maciej Henneberg is quoted by the ABC giving a far more conservative one in 10,000 to 20,000 for an individual carrying both features. Abbott married Robin's granddaughter, Rachel Egan, having met her through the case and suspecting she might be the Somerton Man's great-granddaughter.

The 2022 DNA work ended that theory. The analysis showed no genetic link between Webb and the Thomson line; Robin was found to be the son of Prosper Thomson. Abbott called the finding "a great relief," describing the strain of "not knowing either way." So the shared dental and ear features, on the current evidence, are coincidence rather than inheritance.

This is the correction the article must absorb rather than paper over. If Webb was not Robin's father, then any version of my hypothesis that depends on biological paternity is dead on arrival. What survives is narrower and, I think, more defensible: the search-for-Jessica reading has to rest on what Webb may have *believed* or fixated upon, not on what was true. People act on their convictions, not on later DNA results. A man who believed a former lover in Adelaide had borne his child, or who simply fixated on a woman from his past, would have had a motive to look for her regardless of biology. That is the only footing the motive can now stand on, and I want to be clear that it is a softer footing than the one the theory used to enjoy.

What sits on the other side of the ledger is physical. Her telephone number, X3239, was on the book torn to produce the slip in his pocket, and her door was about 400 metres from where he died. No evidence of comparable weight points anywhere else.

There is no documented meeting between Webb and Jessica, and I will not invent one. But the geography leaves a narrow window worth recording. Jessica left Sydney in 1946 and lived for a period at Mentone, in Melbourne's south-east, before moving to Adelaide in early 1947. Webb was across town in South Yarra throughout. And the number itself was not private: Prosper Thomson, the hire-car dealer Jessica later married, ran vehicles between Melbourne and Adelaide and printed X3239 in his newspaper classifieds from at least July 1947. Anyone answering a Thomson car advertisement held that number. As a route from Webb to Jessica that is conjecture, but it is the one route with a paper trail.

## The evening of 30 November and the physical trail

The movements that can be reconstructed are thin but real. Webb is believed to have arrived in Adelaide by overnight train. He bought a second-class ticket for the 10:50 am train to Henley Beach but never used it. Between roughly 11:00 and 11:15 am he checked a brown suitcase into the Adelaide Railway Station cloakroom, then bought a bus ticket on a service departing from North Terrace, opposite the station, toward Glenelg. The suitcase, recovered on 14 January 1949, was tied to him by a distinctive orange waxed thread that matched a repair in the trousers he died in.

  <figcaption>The suitcase left at the Adelaide Railway Station cloakroom on 30 November 1948. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.</figcaption>
</figure>

Then a gap of several hours that nobody has filled, followed by the witness sightings on Somerton beach that evening and the discovery of the body the next morning. The autopsy found acute gastric haemorrhage, deep congestion of the liver and spleen, a spleen about three times normal size, and a last meal of a pasty eaten three to four hours before death. The pathologist was convinced the death was not natural and suspected a barbiturate or soluble hypnotic, but no poison was identified. The "Tamám Shud" slip was found rolled in a fob pocket months later, and the *Rubáiyát* it came from surfaced when a man reported it had been tossed into his car near Glenelg around the time of the death. On the inside back cover were a telephone number, by some accounts a second number that was never identified, and the five lines of letters.

Two physical details discipline any geographic theory. The body lay roughly 400 metres south of Jessica Thomson's address. And the address itself is on the public record from the 1948 electoral roll as 90A Moseley Street, Glenelg, not 52 Moseley Street as some secondary accounts state. If Webb was looking for her, he died within easy walking distance of her door.

## The code, and the one thing the experts agreed on

Here is the transcription I work from, which is the version most consistently reproduced from the police photograph:

```
WRGOABABD
MLIAOI   (struck out)
WTBIMPANETP
MLIABOAIAQC
ITTMTSAMSTGAB
```

<figure>
  <img src="/images/somerton-code-note.jpg" alt="The five lines of letters pencilled in the back of the Rubáiyát" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>The five lines as recorded from the back of the <em>Rubáiyát</em> — a copy of a copy, over-written from faint indentations. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.</figcaption>
</figure>

Every letter in that block is contestable. The pencilled capitals were faint indentations, photographed and then reportedly over-written to make them legible, which means we are arguing about a copy of a copy. The most consequential disputes are at the line starts, where W and M are barely separable, and at scattered letters where A, H, and other forms blur. Some researchers read the first letters of lines 1, 3, and 4 as M rather than W, which materially changes any analysis that leans on the opening letters. I flag this because a theory that hangs on a specific letter being M or W is only as solid as a seventy-five-year-old pencil rubbing.

The second line is struck through. I follow the consensus in treating it as an abandoned attempt or a correction and excluding it from any frequency work. The fact that a near-identical sequence opens line 4 supports reading line 2 as a false start.

On one point the historical experts converged. In 1949 the code was examined by naval cryptographers, with Australia's foremost codebreaker, Captain Eric Nave, among those involved. The conclusion, reported at the time, was that the letters did not behave like an encrypted message with a recoverable key. The naval assessment, as reported in the press, was that the frequency of the letters "corresponded more favourably with the table of frequencies of initial letters of words in English than with any other table," and that "a reasonable explanation would be that the lines were initial letters of words." In plain terms: this is probably an initialism, the first letters of words, not a cipher.

Later University of Adelaide student projects under Abbott reached the same place by a different route. They showed the letter distribution did not match random jottings produced by test subjects, eliminated more than twenty known cipher types, and found the sequence most consistent with the initials of English words. A 2014 analysis by the computational linguist John Rehling agreed it was an initialism but concluded the source text could probably never be recovered.

The initialism finding is the hinge of this whole article. If each letter is the first letter of a word, then the obvious next question is: the first letters of *what*?

## Letters as places: the itinerary hypothesis

My proposal is that the words behind the initials are mostly place names, and that the five lines are a travel record or plan: somewhere Webb went, or meant to go, as he moved around Adelaide on its 1948 transport networks looking for a particular woman.

This is not a rail-only idea, and that is the main change from my earlier piece. Adelaide in 1948 was a multimodal city. The Glenelg line, converted from steam railway to electric tramway in 1929, was the surviving spine of a tram network that otherwise dwindled through the 1950s. The Municipal Tramways Trust ran an extensive bus fleet alongside the trams, including services from the Adelaide Railway Station out to Glenelg and on to Somerton Park, operated with double-deck diesel buses. South Australian Railways ran the suburban lines, including the Brighton coastal line south past Marino and the Henley Beach line to the west. A man searching Adelaide would have used trains, trams, buses, and his feet interchangeably. So the candidate location for each letter should be anywhere reachable by any of those modes, not just a station on a railway map.

I call the analytical method Radial Proximity Frequency Analysis, or RPFA. The logic is simple. If Webb was working outward from a base while searching, the letters he wrote should over-represent the initials of places within practical travelling distance of that base, and under-represent the initials of places far away. So you compare the letter frequencies in the code against three reference distributions: ordinary written English, the initials of place names across the whole network, and the initials of place names clustered around a candidate search area. A better fit to the local cluster than to either of the others is at least consistent with a localised search.

When I ran the code against railway-station initials rather than standard English, the fit improved markedly: a chi-squared statistic of about 103 against English fell to about 67 against station initials. That is better, not good. It tells you the letters look more like place initials than like prose, which is a weak signal pointing in the right direction, not a proof.

Some specific frequencies are suggestive. The letter B appears at almost exactly the rate you would expect from place initials. The letter A is wildly over-represented in the code relative to place names generally, which would fit a single central location, most obviously Adelaide, being written down repeatedly. Three of the four scored lines may begin with the same letter, which would fit a base location returned to at the start of each leg. I want to stress the conditional mood there. Every one of those observations depends on transcription choices I have already admitted are shaky.

In my earlier analysis I named Marino, a coastal suburb south of Glenelg on the Brighton line, as the likely base, partly on the strength of an M-initial reading of those line starts. I still think Marino is a defensible candidate, and I will note in passing that Marino is the correct 1948 station on that coastal line; the nearby Marion station did not open until 1954 and has no business in any 1948 analysis. But I now hold the base-location claim much more loosely, for reasons the next section makes clear.

## The Q problem and Queenstown

The single most useful thing the multimodal turn did was solve a problem that the rail-only version could not.

The letter Q appears in line 4. Under a rail-only constraint, Q is close to intractable. The only Q-initial station in the entire South Australian rail network is Quorn, roughly 200 miles north of Adelaide in the Flinders Ranges, which is absurd as a stop on a local search. A rail-only reading either has to discard the Q or accept a destination that makes no sense.

Widen the lens to all transport modes and a credible candidate appears within the metropolitan area: Queenstown, a suburb of Port Adelaide. Queenstown sits inside the area a searcher could plausibly cover. Its relevance is not by tram, because the isolated Port Adelaide tram system, which served the Queenstown area, closed in July 1935. Its relevance is by motor bus. There is a documented private bus service connecting the Findon tram terminus to Queenstown that, by one transport-history source, began on 22 September 1941, running via Tapleys Hill Road and Broad Street to a Queenstown terminus.

I want to be careful here, because this is exactly the sort of convenient fact that a theory-builder should distrust. That September 1941 commencement date comes from an enthusiast transport-history compilation, not from a Municipal Tramways Trust record or a contemporary timetable, and I have not been able to verify it against a primary source. Until someone does, the right status for "Queenstown was bus-accessible in 1948" is *probable but unconfirmed*. The position I will defend is that the multimodal frame makes Q solvable in principle, and Queenstown is the only sensible candidate, but the specific evidentiary prop under it has not been nailed down.

## The ending: GAB

Line 5 ends in the letters GAB. Read as place initials, the most natural local reading is Glenelg, Adelaide, Brighton, three points strung along the coast and the routes that connect them. I read this, tentatively, as a trace of Webb's final movements: the Glenelg area where Jessica lived and where he died, the city he kept returning to, and the Brighton coastal corridor that links them.

Brighton here is the coastal suburb on the Marino line, a short walk down the seafront from where the body was found; it is the B that belongs to this grid. Bute, sometimes floated as a B candidate on the strength of the Dorothy theory, is 144 kilometres away on a different network, reached only by changing trains at Snowtown, and would wrench the line off its own map. Keeping the reading coastal is not a convenience; it is what the documented final-day movements demand.

I am deliberately not dressing this up with the lurid geometric labels that circulate online. It is a three-letter sequence at the end of a line that is consistent with a coastal cluster around the place Webb died. That is all I am claiming, and even that is downstream of the assumption that the letters are places at all.

## The problem with a test that cannot fail

Now the part my first draft skated over, and the part that matters most.

I went looking, methodically, for whether each distinct letter in the code has at least one plausible Adelaide-area location behind it. The transcription I use contains seventeen distinct letters. Every single one of them has at least one credible Adelaide-reachable candidate location. On the face of it that sounds like vindication. It is the opposite. It is a warning.

Here is why. The pool of place names reachable around Adelaide by train, tram, bus, and foot is enormous, and the verified station database I work from alone runs to 575 entries, before you add tram stops, bus-served localities, and suburbs. (That database deliberately includes the cross-border stations on the 1938 Railways Commissioner's map — Serviceton in Victoria, Cockburn and Broken Hill in New South Wales, Alice Springs in the Northern Territory — because a complete network layer should not stop at the state line.) With a candidate pool that large, *every* letter of the alphabet that appears in the code will find a match. A random string of seventeen distinct letters would also "match" a set of Adelaide locations, because there is almost no letter you cannot satisfy. Set-membership matching, checking whether each letter has *some* location behind it, cannot fail. A test that cannot fail proves nothing.

This is the line between a hypothesis that is merely *consistent* with the evidence and one that has *survived an attempt to falsify it*. Right now the itinerary hypothesis is only the former. It fits the facts, but so would many things, including chance. The frequency work nudges the odds a little, but a chi-squared improvement from "very poor" to "still poor" is not a result you should build a conviction on.

So what would a real test look like? It has to stop asking whether the letters *can* be places and start asking whether they describe a *coherent journey*. That means building a proper 1948 network layer — every station, tram stop, and bus route, with the connections between them and the modes that served each — then testing whether the *order* of the letters corresponds to a route you could actually have travelled. The right foundation for the rail portion is the primary cartographic source, the 1938 South Australian Railways Commissioner's map (PP No. 47, Schedule of Railways June 30th 1938, signed by R. H. Chapman), with the tram and bus layers added on top. If a line maps onto a sequence of places that connect on the real 1948 network in the order written, the hypothesis has survived something. If the letters only "work" when you reorder them freely and pick whichever of several candidate towns is convenient for each initial, then I am just drawing pictures in clouds. The sequence-and-connectivity constraint is the falsification mechanism. I have not yet built the network layer rigorously enough to run it, and until I do, I will not claim more than consistency.

There is a second, independent test sitting in the physical evidence, anchored on Webb's three documented locations: the railway station, the bus toward Glenelg, and the beach. We have tickets: an unused Henley Beach rail ticket and a used Glenelg-direction bus ticket. We have a suitcase deposited at a known time, witness sightings, and a body location. If the letter sequence records *executed* movements, it should be reconcilable with those anchor points; if it records a *plan*, it may diverge from them in instructive ways. Cross-referencing the code against the ticket stubs and sightings could, in principle, tell us whether we are reading a diary or a wish-list. That, too, is work I have not finished.

## The rivals, weighed fairly

A theory is only as good as its showing against the alternatives. Three deserve serious treatment and one deserves dismissal.

The espionage reading dominated the case for decades, and the context was not absurd: the early Cold War, the Woomera rocket range and Radium Hill within reach of Adelaide, the founding of ASIO in 1949, and the eerie precedent of George Marshall, found dead in Ashton Park, Mosman, in June 1945 with a *Rubáiyát* near him. Jessica Thomson's reported reluctance and her daughter's later claims kept the spy story alive. I think the Webb identification badly weakens it. Webb was a Melbourne instrument maker with a failed marriage, a documented suicide attempt, and no trace of intelligence connections. The spy reading now has to explain why a tradesman with a death-obsessed poetry habit was a foreign agent, and it cannot.

The suicide reading is strong and I do not contest the manner of death so much as accommodate it. "Tamám Shud," meaning "ended" or "finished," torn from the final page of a book about living before the dark, is a plausible final statement from a man whose wife said death was "his greatest desire" and who had tried to kill himself with ether two years earlier. The organ congestion is consistent with poisoning. None of that conflicts with my hypothesis. A man can search for someone and then end his life when the search fails. The itinerary and the suicide are compatible; the letters are about how he spent his last days, not about how they ended.

The horse-betting reading is the one that genuinely competes with mine, and I have to concede it openly. Webb bet on horses; the divorce record establishes the habit. Abbott's own view is that the code probably represents the names of horses Webb backed. And, crucially, the betting reading benefits from exactly the same statistical finding I lean on: initialism. Lists of racehorse names are precisely the kind of thing a punter abbreviates to initials. So the strongest evidence for "these are initials" supports my rival as much as it supports me. Where I think the itinerary reading has a slight edge is the GAB ending and the cluster of coastal initials near the death site, which read more naturally as places than as horses, and the simple fact that a man who travelled across a strange city searching for someone has an obvious reason to keep a record of where he had been. But I cannot claim this is settled. On current evidence, horse-betting and travel-itinerary are roughly co-equal initialism hypotheses, and an intellectually serious reader should hold both.

The "meaningless scribble" possibility is the one I am comfortable setting low, precisely because the 1949 naval analysis and the later Adelaide work both found the letter distribution unlike random output. Not impossible, but the least supported of the options.

## Where this leaves me

I will state my confidence levels rather than hide behind prose.

Webb's identity as the Somerton Man: established, pending only formal coronial confirmation. I would put this very high.

Webb travelling to South Australia in search of a woman: a reasonable inference, and the documented thread points to Glenelg. Her number on the book torn to make the slip in his pocket, and his death 400 metres from her door, are contemporaneous and physical. The rival claim, that he came for his estranged wife in Bute, has no 1948 documentation behind it and a 1949 Melbourne electoral roll against it. I hold the search motive at "plausible," with Jessica as the evidenced focus and Webb's reason for fixing on her unknown.

The paternity belief as the engine of a search for Jessica: this is the weakest link, and the 2022 DNA result forces it down to speculation grounded in circumstantial evidence, salvageable only as something Webb may have *believed*, never as something that was true.

The code as a record of locations: an interesting hypothesis that is consistent with the evidence and modestly supported by frequency analysis, but which has *not* survived a genuine falsification test and cannot be said to until the sequence-and-connectivity work is done. Set-membership matching confirms it too easily to be trusted.

What would change my mind, in either direction, is concrete. Build the 1948 multimodal network layer and find that the letter order traces a travellable route, and the hypothesis graduates from "consistent" to "tested." Build it and find the order is incoherent on the real network, and I will drop the itinerary reading and lean toward the betting list. Verify the Queenstown bus date against a primary source and the Q stops being a soft spot; fail to, and it stays a flag. Produce a 1948 record of Dorothy in South Australia and the Bute question reopens; none exists today. Find a ticket stub or sighting that contradicts the sequence and we learn whether we are reading a plan or a record.

The discipline this case demands is the discipline of any good investigation. The Somerton Man was identified not by the cleverest theory but by the most patient evidence, and the people closest to it, Abbott above all, had to watch a beautiful hypothesis about his own family die under a DNA result. That is the lesson I take from it. Pattern-matching is seductive precisely because it always succeeds; the alphabet is generous and the map is crowded, and you can find a route to almost anywhere if you are willing to choose your towns to suit your letters. The test of a theory is not whether it fits. It is whether it could have failed and did not. Until the letters in that book survive that test, the most I will say is that a lonely man may have written down where he had been looking, and that he was looking near the place he died.
