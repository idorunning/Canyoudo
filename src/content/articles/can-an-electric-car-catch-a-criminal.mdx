The most important part of the Government's 2025 response on phasing out new petrol and diesel vehicles was not the headline date. It was the small print. Cars, vans and specialist vehicles will not be treated in the same way.

That matters to policing. A marked response car, an armed response vehicle and a public-order van may sit in the same force fleet, but they do different work and can fall into different regulatory categories. There is no blanket exemption for the police. There is also no requirement for every police vehicle to become electric in 2030.

The sensible conclusion is less dramatic than either version. Forces should electrify the roles that already suit electric vehicles, use the period to 2035 to deal with the difficult ones, and stop procuring a mixed operational fleet as though one car can do every job.

<h2 id="what-the-rules-say">What the rules actually say</h2>

The [April 2025 joint government response](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/phasing-out-sales-of-new-petrol-and-diesel-cars-from-2030-and-supporting-the-zev-transition/outcome/phasing-out-sales-of-new-petrol-and-diesel-cars-from-2030-and-supporting-the-zev-transition-summary-of-responses-and-joint-government-response) sets out four points relevant to a police fleet.

From 2030, ordinary new cars powered only by petrol or diesel are due to leave the market. Battery-electric cars, full hybrids and plug-in hybrids may still be sold. New vans are treated differently: petrol and diesel vans, as well as hybrids and electric vans, may continue to be sold between 2030 and 2035. The stated end point is that all new cars and vans will be zero emission from 2035.

Existing vehicles are not ordered off the road on either date. Nor does the policy prevent the sale of used combustion vehicles. A force could extend the life of a diesel car or buy used, although doing so would bring its own problems with reliability, maintenance and residual value.

There is an exemption from the 2030 car rule for *special-purpose vehicles*. This is where the wording can mislead. In the consultation, respondents referred to emergency vehicles as a reason for an exemption. The Government then tied the exemption to the existing type-approval definition in [Annex I of Regulation 2018/858](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2018/858/annex/I/part/A/division/5), which includes ambulances, wheelchair-accessible vehicles and armoured vehicles.

That is not the same as exempting every police car. A standard response vehicle does not become a special-purpose vehicle because it has Battenburg markings, blue lights, a radio and a computer. An ARV with anti-bullet armour may fall within the armoured-vehicle category; an unarmoured SUV carrying firearms officers should not be assumed to do so. The registration and type approval of the finished conversion will matter.

There is another qualification. The current [Vehicle Emissions Trading Scheme](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a4233648cbc951a27d80d99/vets-how-to-comply.pdf) is already law and controls the proportion of non-zero-emission vehicles that manufacturers register. The detailed 2030 and 2035 phase-out rules described in the government response still require further legislation. As at August 2026, it is too strong to say that a force is already legally forbidden from buying a particular car in 2031. It would be equally careless to write a fleet strategy on the assumption that the policy will disappear.

<PullQuote>Police vehicles are not exempt as a class. The vehicle category and the conversion decide the position, not the crest on the door.</PullQuote>

<h2 id="across-the-uk">Will this affect police forces across the UK?</h2>

Yes. The ZEV mandate has applied across the United Kingdom since Northern Ireland joined the scheme in January 2025. Manufacturer targets are enforced as a UK-wide average rather than separately in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland. The post-2030 policy is also intended to operate UK-wide.

The mechanism matters. The rules primarily control what manufacturers may register, not what an individual chief constable may order. By 2030, 80% of a manufacturer's new cars and 70% of its new vans are scheduled to be zero emission. Combustion models will therefore become fewer, less attractive for manufacturers to develop and potentially more expensive before any purchasing prohibition bites.

Police Scotland, the PSNI or a Welsh force cannot rely on a different showroom. All forces will draw from the same shrinking pool of suitable cars and conversions. Larger vehicles outside the M1 car and N1 light-van categories sit under different regimes, so each force also needs to know how its converted vans, minibuses and heavier vehicles are actually registered.

<h2 id="cars-for-roles">Start with the role, not the fuel</h2>

The usual fleet debate asks whether an electric car can replace a diesel car. That is the wrong unit of analysis. The question is whether a particular electric vehicle can cover a particular duty cycle, including the worst credible shift rather than the average one.

| Police role | Sensible position now | What must be proved |
|---|---|---|
| Neighbourhood, enquiries, supervision and general duties | Battery electric by default | Daily mileage, home-station dwell time and access to overnight charging |
| Urban and suburban response | Electric where telematics support it | Back-to-back shifts, time away from base, winter use and a rapid-charge fallback |
| Rural response | Long-range electric trials, with hybrid as a bridge where necessary | Area covered, mutual-aid journeys, charging geography and the 95th-percentile shift |
| Roads policing and pursuit | High-performance EV trials; hybrid retained during the transition | Sustained speed, starting charge, tyre and brake performance, and immediate redeployment |
| Armed response | No presumption either way | Full laden weight, crew and kit payload, protection level, pursuit requirement and 24/7 readiness |
| Cell, dog, public-order and equipment vans | Decide conversion by conversion | Laden range, heating and ventilation loads, payload, idle time and vehicle category |

This approach will produce a mixed fleet for a while. That is not policy failure. It is what happens when capability is taken seriously.

Most response runs are short. Electric cars are quick from low speed, efficient in stop-start traffic and far better than a diesel at sitting on a cordon with lights and radios running. Pursuit is the awkward tail. At high speed, aerodynamic demand rises sharply; the important number is not the car's advertised range but its charge when the pursuit begins.

The armed response problem is different. It is as much about weight as energy. Crew, body armour, weapons, method-of-entry equipment, medical kit, racking, communications and the conversion itself all consume payload. A large battery consumes it too.

The answer is not to exclude ARVs from electrification indefinitely. It is to create a national test specification and ask manufacturers to meet it. Forty-three separate forces placing small bespoke orders will not pull a suitable vehicle into the market. A credible national requirement might.

<h2 id="range-in-2035">What range should policing expect in 2035?</h2>

Fleet plans written around the electric cars of 2020 will be wrong. Production technology has already moved faster than many police strategies.

The 2026 BMW iX3 offers up to **500 miles WLTP**, uses cells with 20% greater energy density than BMW's previous generation and can add up to **231 miles of rated range in ten minutes** from a suitable 400 kW charger. At the other end of the market, current fleet-sized electric cars still sit nearer 250 to 350 miles. Electric vans lag behind: current Peugeot models range from about **205 miles for the E-Partner** to **261 miles for the larger E-Boxer**, before conversion, load and winter conditions take their share.

Nobody can give a precise 2035 figure without pretending to know manufacturers' product plans. A useful planning assumption is possible, though. By 2035, police-capable cars in the ordinary fleet market are likely to offer roughly **400 to 500 miles WLTP**, with longer-range models above that. For operational planning, I would count on about **280 to 400 miles that can safely be used**, depending on speed, temperature, conversion and the reserve a force insists on keeping.

For light vans, a reasonable stress-testing band is **250 to 400 miles WLTP**, but perhaps **180 to 300 miles usable when laden and converted**. Those are planning bands, not promises. A cell van using heating or cooling while stationary and a public-order carrier full of officers and equipment will not return the brochure number.

The more important change may be charging rather than battery size. A ten-minute stop can already add more than 200 rated miles to the right car. By 2035, buying an enormous battery for every vehicle may be a worse use of money and payload than giving the fleet reliable access to rapid charging. But the words *reliable access* are doing the work.

<h2 id="charging">The infrastructure is part of the vehicle</h2>

The UK had **119,080 public chargers**, including **27,372 rated at 50 kW or above**, on 1 April 2026. That network is useful for contingency and long journeys. It should not be the operating model for response policing.

A marked car waiting behind two family SUVs at a motorway service area is unavailable. A public charger can also be broken, blocked or limited to a payment system the officer cannot use. Fast charging access therefore needs to be contracted and designed, not left to an app on the driver's phone.

The Metropolitan Police reached the same conclusion through pilots and telematics. Its April 2026 outline business case sought **£4.1 million** for the next stage of a charging programme built around a combination of on-estate charging and private off-estate hubs. The important part is not the London number. It is the method: use actual vehicle movements to decide where power is needed, then treat charging as operational infrastructure.

A force needs plentiful managed AC charging where vehicles naturally stand, rapid DC charging at the sites that turn cars around, and guaranteed off-estate hubs for vehicles that rarely return to the same station. It also needs live fault monitoring, priority rules for low-charge response cars, roaming payment arrangements and a plan for site or grid failure. Estates, fleet, ICT and operational policing have to own that system together.

<Callout label="A better readiness measure" tone="note">
Do not report only how many electric vehicles or chargers the force owns. Report the proportion of deployable response, roads-policing and armed vehicles above their role-specific minimum state of charge at each shift change. That is the measure the control room will feel.
</Callout>

<h2 id="what-forces-should-do">The decisions cannot wait until 2030</h2>

Every force should be able to classify its fleet by role, duty cycle, payload and return-to-base pattern by the end of the next procurement round. Telematics should show the median, the 95th percentile and the exceptional shifts. Without that, claims that a role is or is not suitable for electric vehicles are opinions.

Battery electric should then become the default for roles the data supports. Exceptions should be written against an operational requirement, reviewed at each replacement cycle and narrowed as the market improves. Hybrids are a useful bridge for rural response, pursuit and perhaps armed roles between 2030 and 2035, but only if they are regularly charged. A plug-in hybrid that is never plugged in is simply a heavier combustion car.

National policing bodies should also settle common specifications for response cars, pursuit vehicles, ARVs and the main van conversions. Payload, charging curve, high-speed endurance, electrical equipment load and winter performance should be tested once and published. Manufacturers respond to credible demand; fragmented experiments create interesting press releases and weak purchasing power.

The government policy does not force every police car to become electric in 2030. It does remove the option of doing nothing. By 2035, range should no longer be the reason most police roles cannot move. The deciding issues will be whether forces bought for the role, installed the power early enough and managed charge with the same seriousness they currently manage fuel. Those are choices policing can make now.
