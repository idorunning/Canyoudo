**The most useful fact in crime detection may be this: people who do big bad things also do little bad things.** A burglar does not stop being a rule-breaker when he gets out of the car, and a serious offender rarely confines his offending to the serious. He parks where he should not, drives when he is banned, skips the insurance, jumps the fare. Each of those minor offences is trivial in itself — and each one is a small flag, raised by the offender himself, saying *look at me*. Self-selection policing is the discipline of noticing the flag.

The approach was developed and championed by [Professor Jason Roach](/influential-people/jason-roach-and-the-art-of-catching-criminals-by-their-small-mistakes) and Professor Ken Pease, who set it out in full in their book [*Self-Selection Policing: Theory, Research and Practice*](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1057/978-1-137-46852-9) (Palgrave Macmillan, 2016). Their phrase for the underlying premise has become the field's shorthand: ["those who do big bad things also do little bad things"](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/stopping-crime/202211/the-self-selection-policing-approach). The job of self-selection policing (SSP) is to identify which "little bad things" are the most reliable signposts to the big ones — and then make sure officers treat those minor offences as an opportunity rather than a nuisance.

### What it actually means

Most proactive policing works from the person to the offence: intelligence suggests someone is an active offender, so police watch them and wait for the crime. Self-selection policing reverses the direction. It works from the offence to the person: a minor offence is committed in plain sight, and the offender — by committing it — *selects himself* for a second look. Nobody had to profile him. Nobody had to guess. As Roach and Pease put it, by committing small offences, active serious criminals are ["offering themselves up for police attention"](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/stopping-crime/202211/the-self-selection-policing-approach).

That reversal matters for two reasons. First, it is **proactive without being speculative** — the trigger is an actual offence, dealt with on its own terms, not a hunch about a person. Second, it is **cheap**. The encounter is already happening: the parking ticket is already being written, the disqualified driver is already stopped. SSP simply asks the officer to spend two more minutes asking who they are dealing with.

### The evidence: disabled bays and disqualified drivers

The founding study is almost charmingly mundane. In Huddersfield in the late 1990s, [Sylvia Chenery, Chris Henshaw and Ken Pease examined cars parked illegally in disabled bays](https://popcenter.asu.edu/sites/default/files/problems/bicycle_theft/PDFs/Chenery_etal_1999.pdf) — published by the Home Office in 1999 as *Illegal Parking in Disabled Bays: A Means of Offender Targeting*. The hypothesis was that the most committed criminals are also the most versatile, unwilling to be bound by law or convention of any kind, right down to where they leave the car. The results bore it out: [around one in four of the vehicles](https://news-archive.hud.ac.uk/news/2015/may/helpingthepolicewiththeirenquiriesresearchaidspolicing.php) parked illicitly in disabled bays was of immediate police interest — registered to people with live warrants, disqualifications or active criminal involvement, at rates far beyond anything a random sample of motorists would produce.

Driving while disqualified turns out to be an even stronger signal. [Roach's study of disqualified drivers](https://academic.oup.com/policing/article-abstract/13/3/300/2951290), published in *Policing: A Journal of Policy and Practice* (13(3)), examined the offending histories of a sample of drivers caught behind the wheel while banned — and found an overwhelming proportion had convictions for other, often serious, offences, with offending patterns closer to mainstream career criminals than to ordinary motoring offenders. The logic is intuitive once stated: a person legally banned from driving who drives anyway is demonstrating, in real time, that court orders do not constrain them.

The wider literature points the same way. Studies in [the UK, the USA, Australia, New Zealand and parts of Europe](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/310517679_Self-Selection_Policing) have repeatedly found links between serious driving offences — drink-driving, dangerous driving, driving while disqualified — and involvement in mainstream violent and acquisitive crime. The "trigger offences" are not arbitrary: they are the minor offences whose commission statistically enriches the pool of people worth a closer look.

### Why it's useful

For a service permanently short of resource, SSP has a rare combination of virtues:

- **It finds active offenders, now.** Conviction records tell you who offended in the past. A trigger offence is being committed in the present, by someone currently willing to break rules — exactly the population current policing most wants to find.
- **It piggybacks on routine work.** No new patrols, no surveillance budget. The contact is already happening; SSP changes what the officer does with it. The marginal cost is curiosity.
- **It is fair in a way profiling is not.** The trigger is conduct, not character. Nobody is checked because of who they are, what they look like, or where they live — only because of an offence they chose to commit, in public, in front of an officer. The offender genuinely does select himself.
- **It complements the rest of the evidence-based toolkit.** [Hot-spots policing](/topics/hotspot-policing) tells you *where* to be; self-selection tells you *who*, among the people you meet there, deserves the second question. It slots naturally into the targeting–testing–tracking framework this site has described in its [profile of Lawrence Sherman](/influential-people/lawrence-sherman-and-the-invention-of-evidence-based-policing).

This site has applied the same lens internally before: [Policing the Police: Why the 'Small Stuff' Matters](/police-policy/policing-the-police-why-the-small-stuff-matters) argued that minor misconduct by officers is the self-selection trigger for later gross misconduct. The symmetry is the point — the insight is about rule-breakers, and it does not stop applying when the rule-breaker wears a uniform.

### The honest limits

An evidence-based explainer owes you the caveats, and there are real ones.

**Base rates still rule.** "One in four of police interest" means three in four are not. A trigger offence justifies a check, never a presumption; the follow-up must be proportionate to what the check actually finds. Treating every disabled-bay parker as a master criminal would be both wrong and counterproductive.

**Triggers must be chosen by evidence, not intuition.** The whole approach collapses into ordinary prejudice the moment "offences that statistically flag serious offenders" drifts into "behaviour that annoys officers". Roach and Pease are explicit that candidate triggers need empirical validation — which minor offences, tested against records, actually enrich for serious offending — and that some intuitively appealing candidates fail the test.

**Implementation is harder than the idea.** [Roach's disqualified-driver work](https://academic.oup.com/policing/article-abstract/13/3/300/2951290) itself concludes that obstacles — data access at the roadside, officer time, recording practice — must be cleared before trigger offences can be systematically embedded in practice. Like most of evidence-based policing, the science is the easy half; the organisational plumbing is the hard half.

**Disproportionality needs watching.** Even conduct-based triggers can fall unevenly if enforcement of the minor offence itself is uneven. A force adopting SSP should track who is being checked, exactly as it should for [stop and search](/topics/stop-and-search).

### What a force would actually do

The practical version is unglamorous, which is rather the appeal. Pick two or three empirically validated trigger offences — driving while disqualified is the best-evidenced candidate. Brief officers that these offences warrant a routine extra check: who is this person, what is attached to them, does anything live come back. Record the outcomes, so the force can test — in the proper, [evidence-based](/topics/evidence-based-policing) sense — whether its triggers are earning their keep, and retire the ones that are not. That last step is what separates self-selection policing from a slogan: it is a hypothesis about which small offences predict big offenders, and hypotheses are for testing.

The deeper lesson generalises beyond any single tactic. Offenders are not specialists, crime is not compartmentalised, and the everyday infractions police see constantly are not noise — they are data. The service that learns to read them gets something close to free intelligence, handed over voluntarily, by the very people it most needs to find.
