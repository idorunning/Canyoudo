## The problem with the phrase

"Evidence-based" is the kind of phrase that's done a lot of work without anyone checking whether it could carry the weight. It appears in policy documents, ministerial speeches, training packages, and the introductory paragraphs of consultation responses. It almost always sounds reasonable. It almost always means something slightly different to each person reading it.

In practice, three things usually masquerade as evidence-based policy.

The first is *somebody-said-so policy*. A senior figure at a conference quoted a study. The study turned out to be a meta-analysis of seven other studies, six of which were under-powered and one of which was conducted in a different jurisdiction. The policy got written anyway.

The second is *plausible-mechanism policy*. The logic seems sound. The intervention should work in theory. Nobody has tested it at scale, but the theory of change document looks tidy, and the pilot evaluation was a survey of the people delivering it.

The third — and this is the one that does the real damage — is *measurement-displacement policy*. Something has been measured, so it must be the thing that matters. The evidence is plentiful, but it's evidence about the wrong question.

## What proper evidence looks like

Genuine evidence-based policy starts with a question that's worth answering, not a programme that's looking for justification. It accepts that the answer might be "we don't know yet". It tolerates findings that are inconvenient. And it builds in some way of knowing whether the change actually worked, rather than treating implementation as the success criterion.

That's harder than it sounds.

## A practical test

When you're reading a policy proposal that claims an evidence base, try three questions.

Is the cited evidence answering the same question the policy is answering? Often it isn't.

Is the evidence strong enough to bear the weight being placed on it? A single pilot in one force is not the same as multiple replications across jurisdictions.

What would the proposers accept as evidence the policy isn't working? If the answer is "nothing realistic", you're not looking at evidence-based policy. You're looking at something else.
