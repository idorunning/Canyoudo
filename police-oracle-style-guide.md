# Police Oracle Style Guide

A second register for *Thinking About Policing*: the hard-news, trade-press
report, modelled on how *Police Oracle* itself is written. It sits
**alongside** `writing-style.md`, not instead of it. That file still governs
vocabulary and the AI-writing tells, for everything on the site, including
pieces written in this register. This file is about when to switch register,
and how a report is put together and worded once you have.

> How to use this with an AI assistant: point it at both files. `writing-style.md`
> wins on banned words, structural tics and AI-tell checks. This file governs
> structure, tense, attribution and layout whenever the piece is a **news
> report** rather than an **essay or argument**.

---

## 1. When to use it

The site has two registers, and switching between them is a decision, not a
mood:

- **The essay** (the existing house voice, per `writing-style.md`): first
  person or confident third person, building to a judgement the writer
  stands behind. "The answer is a qualified yes."
- **The news report** (this guide): what happened, who said what — with the
  writer's own opinion, if there is one, kept in a separate, clearly marked
  Analysis section instead of running through the piece.

Here's the test: read back your strongest sentence. If it's a claim only
you're making — not something a named person said, a document states, or a
figure shows — you've slid from report into argument. In a report, that
sentence either gets a name attached to it ("X has criticised the decision,
calling it…") or it moves to Analysis. A report can still have an edge, but
the edge comes from what's being reported and who said it, not from the
reporter's own adjectives.

Use the news register for: force announcements, HMICFRS or College of
Policing publications, court outcomes, misconduct findings, funding and
policy changes — anything with a "who did what, when" spine. Use the essay
register for explainers and arguments, where the site's own take is the
point.

## 2. Structure

**Inverted pyramid.** Lead with the single most important fact. Every
paragraph after that matters a bit less than the one before it, so the piece
could be cut from the bottom and lose nothing essential. Don't save the key
fact for a reveal — that's essay structure, not news structure.

**Headlines.** Present tense, active voice. Don't drop a verb unless it's a
genuine headline shorthand ("Met officer jailed for…"). Name the force where
it fits. Skip the question-mark headline unless the story is genuinely
unresolved.

**Catchy is fine — clickbait isn't.** Unlike a wire-service headline, this
site's headlines can keep some of the essay register's flavour: a coined
phrase, a colon-and-subtitle, a dash for emphasis. The line to hold is
whether the headline still tells the reader what happened. "Self-Selection
Policing: Catching Serious Criminals — and Bad Cops — Through the Small
Offences They Can't Help Committing" is catchy but accurate — it names the
actual mechanism. A headline that promises a reveal or plays coy about the
story ("The One Thing Every Officer Gets Wrong") is clickbait regardless of
register, and stays banned in both.

**The present-perfect lead.** The opening sentence usually runs in the
present perfect — "A Metropolitan Police officer **has been jailed** for…",
"The College of Policing **has published**…" — because you're reporting
something whose effect is still current, not a closed episode from the past.
Drop into simple past once the lead is set and you're narrating how it got
there.

**The nut graph.** The second or third paragraph answers "why this matters"
in two or three sentences — the figures, the policy it sits inside, the
precedent — before the piece gets into detail. Compressed, not developed;
save the development for the body.

**Attribution runs all the way through, not just in the lead.** Anything
beyond the bare fact of what happened should trace back to a named source:
the force's statement, a judge's remarks, a watchdog's report, a named
critic. If a report has no attributed sentences past paragraph two, it's
drifting toward essay.

---

## 3. Language

**Tense.** Present perfect for the lead and anything still live at
publication ("has said it will review the policy"). Simple past for
sequence and history once you've set the scene. Present tense only for
standing facts ("the force covers a population of…") — never for the news
event itself.

**The attribution principle.** This is the whole mechanism: a report can
carry a criticism, a doubt, an implication — without the reporter making it
— by routing it through a quote or a named source. "The watchdog said the
force's response fell short" reports a criticism; it doesn't make one. If a
criticism can't be pinned to anyone — the force, a watchdog, a lawyer, a
figure — it doesn't belong in the report. It goes in Analysis, clearly
labelled.

**Default to "said."** Use it for reported speech unless a stronger verb is
plainly accurate — "confirmed," "denied," "ruled," "ordered." Steer clear of
verbs that sneak in a reading of the speaker's tone or motive: "admitted,"
"claimed," "insisted," "conceded" all import the reporter's judgement of
what was said. If the tone matters, quote it and let the words do the work.

**Sentence craft.** Shorter and flatter than the essay register. One fact
per sentence where you can manage it. Subordinate clauses carry context, not
editorial colour. Skip the essay register's long argumentative sentence that
resolves on a blunt closer — that rhythm belongs to the other file.

**British English throughout**, as elsewhere on the site: organise,
behaviour, defence, sceptical, whilst where it's natural, "£", British
ranks (PC, Sergeant, Chief Constable).

## 4. Quotes

Quotes do the editorialising in this register, because a quote is attributed
by definition. Use them to carry:

- **Criticism the reporter can't make directly.** "It beggars belief that
  this was allowed to continue," said [named critic] — the report is
  reporting an attack, not launching one.
- **The commercial-interest tell.** When a source has skin in the game, let
  the quote and the attribution show it rather than flagging it with an
  adjective. "Avigilon, which sells the cameras under review, said the
  technology 'meets all current standards'" — the reader draws the
  inference from who's speaking, not from the reporter calling the claim
  self-serving. Naming the speaker's interest in the attribution line does
  the job an editorial aside would otherwise do clumsily.
- **A figure or exact wording a paraphrase would flatten.** Quote a legal
  test, a use-of-force threshold, an exact percentage, when the precision
  itself is the point.

Keep quotes short and worth their place. Don't quote someone just repeating
a fact the reporter's own sentence already gave. A quote earns its slot by
adding something — a judgement, a justification, a number, a tone — that the
reporter's own prose isn't allowed to supply.

## 5. On-page furniture

**Functional crossheads, not essayistic chapter titles.** The essay register
uses argumentative, sentence-style headings ("Why we will not say
standard"). The news register uses short, plain labels that get a skimming
reader through a longer piece: "Background," "The investigation," "Force
response," "What happens next." They describe the section; they don't argue
a point.

**Standfirst.** One bold sentence under the headline, stating the core fact
plainly — not a teaser, not a question, not the essay register's opening
scene. A reader who stops there should know what happened.

**Pull quote.** Pulled from an attributed quote in the piece, never from the
reporter's own prose — this register has no lines of its own worth pulling
out, by design.

**The quarantined Analysis section.** Where the piece needs the site's own
view — the wider significance, a prediction, a criticism the reporting
itself can't pin on anyone — it goes in a clearly labelled "Analysis"
section, set apart from the report above it, so the reader always knows
which mode they're in. Don't let opinion leak up into the report, and don't
let the report's flat factual register leak down and dilute Analysis's
licence to actually argue something.

## 6. What breaks the register

- **First person.** The reporter's "I" doesn't appear in a news report.
  (It's welcome, per `writing-style.md`, in the essay register — the two
  files aren't in conflict, they just cover different pieces.)
- **Loaded verbs.** "Admitted," "claimed," "insisted," "boasted,"
  "conceded," "was forced to" — each one reads the speaker's credibility or
  reluctance for the reader. Use "said," or a plain, accurate verb.
- **Adjectival judgement.** "A shocking failure," "a damning report," "a
  worrying rise" — the adjective is doing a job a named source or a figure
  should be doing. If it's shocking, show the figure or quote whoever called
  it that; don't call it that yourself.
- **The AI tells still apply.** Everything in `writing-style.md`'s "Words
  and phrases to avoid" and "Structural tics" lists holds here too —
  inflated adjectives, hedging throat-clearing, the corrective antithesis,
  tricolons everywhere, over-bolding, tidy moralising closers. This register
  has its own extra failure mode on top: it doesn't fail by sounding like
  AI, it fails by sounding like an opinion column dressed as a report.

---

## 7. Pre-publish checklist

1. Does the lead report the single most important fact, in the present
   perfect?
2. Does the nut graph give the wider context in two or three sentences?
3. Is every claim past the bare facts attributed to a named source?
4. Has every "admitted / claimed / insisted / boasted" been swapped for
   "said" or a plain, accurate verb?
5. Has every adjective doing editorial work (shocking, damning, worrying)
   been swapped for the figure or quote that would justify it?
6. Do the crossheads function as labels, not arguments?
7. Is the standfirst a plain statement of the core fact, not a tease?
8. Is the site's own view confined to a clearly marked Analysis section,
   with nothing leaking either way across that line?
9. Does the piece still pass everything in `writing-style.md` — banned
   words, structural tics, British English?

---

## Notes and caveats

- **Two files, one house.** `writing-style.md` governs vocabulary,
  structural tics and AI-tell checks across both registers. This file
  governs register and format: when to write a report instead of an essay,
  and how to build one once you have. Neither file overrides the other; a
  piece in the news register still has to pass `writing-style.md`.
- **The register is a spectrum in practice**, not a hard switch — the
  site's existing "frontline guide" format (Civil Orders, Martyn's Law)
  already borrows some report-style furniture (comparison tables,
  `PowerSummary` boxes) inside an essay. Use judgement: a genuine hard-news
  event (a court outcome, a force announcement, a watchdog report) belongs
  in this register; a practitioner's how-to belongs in the essay register
  with report-style furniture borrowed where useful.
