Three directions, and the twelve things all of them kill

The site today is competent and coherent — the "Evidence Desk" system does hold together. But almost every one of its signature moves is a move that a generated site makes. Below is the audit, then the engineering that would make the rebuild genuinely hard to copy, then three complete directions.

What reads as generated

01The masked hairline grid behind the heroThe single most recognisable generated-site tell of the last two years. It signals "technical" without carrying any information.
02Plus Jakarta Sans as the display faceIn the same family of defaults as Inter and Space Grotesk. Extrabold at −0.035em tracking is the house style of every AI landing page.
03Tailwind teal (#0f766e) as the accentStraight off the default palette, and it fights the navy logo. The brand currently contradicts itself in the masthead.
04Rounded card + hairline + 3px hover liftGeneric SaaS card grammar. Every article gets an identical 280px box, so nothing is ranked — hierarchy is thrown away.
05Pill CTAs with a trailing arrow"Explore the tools →". The arrow adds no information; the pill belongs to a product, not a publication.
06Pulsing dots on "Live" and "Recently published"Urgency theatre. A date is more informative and more honest than an animated dot.
07Kicker → giant headline → standfirst → stat lineThe default hero composition. Half the first screen is empty and no article is visible on the left.
08Gradient-masked carousel rowsNetflix rows for a 32-article archive. Horizontal scroll hides the body of work instead of showing it.
09Stock photography as mood tileImages should be evidence with a caption and a source, or not appear at all.
10"Put the evidence to work"Product-marketing voice on a research publication. The site should name things, not sell them.
11"Evidence" three times in the first screenKicker, strapline and section label all say it. Repetition drains the word of the meaning it is meant to carry.
12A 612 KB raster logo with soft edgesBlurred at every size, unusable in one colour, and half a megabyte on every page load. It should be a few hundred bytes of SVG.

What makes it technically impressive

Not effects — restraint plus modern platform features that a template cannot fake. Everything listed here is used in the mockups on this page, so the lab is its own proof.

light-dark()One palette definition carries both themes. Replaces every duplicated .dark block in global.css — a single color-scheme flip retints the whole site.
oklch() + color-mix()Tints and hover states derived from the accent rather than hand-picked, so the palette stays perceptually even and a retune is one value.
grid-template-columns: subgridThe article index aligns reference, title and figures on shared column lines. Real tabular alignment, not nested flexbox approximating it.
@container / cqw unitsCards and rows respond to their own column, not the viewport — which is why the mockups above reflow correctly inside this frame.
animation-timeline: view()Reading progress and scroll reveals move off the main thread and out of JavaScript entirely. Deletes the ReadingProgress listener.
@view-transition + view-transition-nameAlready enabled site-wide; extend it so a headline in the index morphs into the article headline rather than cross-fading.
text-wrap: balance / prettyNo orphaned last words in headlines, no single-word final lines in standfirsts. Typesetting the browser now does natively.
font-variant-numericTabular, slashed-zero figures everywhere digits stack — dates, counts, read times. Columns line up because the numerals do.
popover + anchor positioningFootnote previews become native popovers anchored to their reference, replacing the hand-rolled positioning in FootnotePreview.astro.
content-visibility: autoBelow-fold sections skip layout and paint until scrolled to. Measurable on a 32-entry index page.

How to read the three directions

Each is a complete homepage using the site's real articles, real section names and real figures — 32 articles, 22 topics, 374 minutes. Each carries its own palette, type pairing, logo mark and copy revisions. They are not variations on one idea; pick a direction, or tell me which parts of which to cross.

1 · Command Paper — institutional and authoritative. Reads like a marked-up policy document.
2 · The Instrument — Swiss and measured. The homepage is a data instrument, not a brochure.
3 · Night Watch — dark, editorial, quiet. Type at extreme scale carries everything.

Command Paper

Direction 01

Grounded in the materials of British official publishing — recycled grey-green stock, Hansard bottle green for structure, command red held back for corrections and live states. The layout signature is the marginal apparatus: a left rail on every block carrying reference, section and read time, the way an annotated policy paper reads. No cards anywhere; hierarchy comes from rule weight.

Lead paper

Can an Electric Car Catch a Criminal?

The physics, the payload and the pursuit — a guide for the people signing the fleet forms.

British policing has promised to electrify a fleet that is four-fifths diesel. The pursuit car, the armed response vehicle and the 3am rural shift are governed by physics that a procurement target cannot waive.

What follows is not an argument for or against the transition. It is an attempt to match the car to the role, to fund the charging before the vehicle, and to stop a single successful demonstration from writing fleet policy for a decade.

The Instrument

Direction 02

The most characteristic thing about this site is that it measures things — so the hero is the publication record plotted on a real scale, not a headline about evidence. White ground, black rules, a single cobalt reserved strictly for interaction. Every article is an indexed entry in a subgrid-aligned table with tabular figures. The riskiest and the most distinctive of the three.

On the visible grid: kill 01 was the masked hairline grid — a decorative texture that fades out radially and that nothing aligns to. This one is the real twelve-column grid, drawn at full strength with no mask, and every element on the panel sits on it. Drawing your own grid is a Swiss convention; fading a fake one behind a headline is a generated-site tell. If it still reads as the same move to you, it comes out.

Night Watch

Direction 03

Dark by commitment, not as a mode. The ground is a cold blue-black and the only accent is sodium — the orange of British street lighting at 3am, which is when most of this work is read. No cards, no panels, no glow, no gradient: hierarchy is carried entirely by type scale, from a 10px label to a 96px headline. The quietest of the three, and the one that would look most expensive.

Share cards: what a link looks like when someone posts it

The meta tags are already right — summary_large_image, an absolute twitter:image, 1200×630. What is wrong is the picture itself. Every one of the 32 articles unfurls as the same typographic card, in a palette that appears nowhere else on the site, and eleven of the underlying hero images are too small to make a sharp card even if we wanted to use them.

Three problems, in order of cost

01Every article looks identical in a feedThe generated card carries only the title. A reader scrolling X sees the same black rectangle whether the piece is about nitazenes or the Somerton Man. Nothing earns a second look.
02The card uses a third, unrelated paletteThe card is cream on near-black with an oxblood rule (#7C2828). The site is teal. The logo is navy. Three brands, none of which agree — and the card is the one most people see first.
03Eleven source images are too small for a sharp cardBelow 1200px wide there is nothing to crop from. Type C is the honest answer for those, rather than upscaling a 512px photo into a 1200px card.

The card system

Three card types, chosen by what the article actually has rather than applied uniformly. Each retints to whichever direction wins — the type, palette and mark below are the only things that change.

Type A · Photograph

When the hero is a real photograph ≥1200px that survives a 2:1 crop

Type B · Portrait split

Influential People — a face performs in a feed, and a 2:1 crop destroys it

Type C · Typographic

Charts, diagrams, logos, and anything without a usable photograph

The rule

Card type is derived at build time, so no article has to remember to set anything:

hero is a photo, ≥1200px wide, landscape → Type ASmart-cropped to 1200×630 with sharp's attention strategy, which centres on the region of highest entropy rather than the geometric middle. A police car stays in frame; a sky does not fill the card.
section is Influential People and a portrait exists → Type BThe portrait is never cropped to 2:1 — it keeps its own aspect in the right-hand third. Faces stop being decapitated by the crop.
anything else → Type CCharts, SVG diagrams, company logos and stock filler all fall through to the typographic card, which is honest: there is no photograph worth showing.
frontmatter shareImage overrides all of itOne optional field for the cases where the automatic choice is wrong — a sensitive image, a better frame further down the article, a card you want to hand-make.

What every article gets, and what needs fixing first

All 32 articles, audited against their current hero. Eleven are under 1200px wide and two are portrait-oriented, so a 2:1 crop loses most of the frame.

Those files are absent from a fresh checkout but present on the deployed site, so any audit run against the working tree alone will wrongly report them missing.

Already changed

These are direction-independent, so they are in the branch now rather than waiting on a decision:

name="twitter:card"The four Twitter tags used property=. X's parser reads name=; it was falling back to the Open Graph tags every time.
