Part one

A logo has two jobs, and the one I drew did neither

You were right that it was odd. Here is the standard I should have held it to in the first place, then three marks tested against it properly — at 16 pixels, in one colour, on every ground the site actually uses.

What a logo is for

Job one

Recognition

It must be identifiable at 16 pixels in a browser tab, in a single colour, reversed on dark, and at the corner of a share card in someone's feed. A mark with four separate elements has none of those. That was the first failure: a box, a crossbar, a stem and a red tick is three too many things.

Job two

Explanation

It should say something true about what this is, in a way a stranger can decode without a caption. "A page rule with a correction stroke in the margin" is a private joke — I knew what it meant because I wrote it. Nobody arriving cold gets there. If a mark needs its own paragraph, it has failed.

Also true of the existing PNG

The one on the site now

A soft-edged navy TP inside a broken ring, 612 KB, with gradient strokes that blur at every size and collapse to mush in one colour. It is also navy while the site is teal, so the two halves of the brand disagree in the masthead.

Three candidates

Tested, not just drawn
Candidate A

The citation bracket

Recommended

What it explains. Square brackets are the citation mark — [1], [ibid], [emphasis added]. They are the one piece of punctuation that means sourced. Putting the T inside them says, without a caption, that this is a publication which shows its working. It is also the only candidate that keeps working as a system rather than only as a mark.

Why it is recognisable. Two vertical strokes and a serif T give a symmetrical five-stroke rhythm — a distinct silhouette, where a bare T has none. A bare serif T is the most generic letterform there is, and already belongs to a newspaper and a blogging platform.

Where it struggles, honestly. Below about 20 pixels the brackets and the serifs start to close up, which you can see in the 16px specimen below. That is normal and it has a normal fix: a simplified favicon variant, which is what the Guardian and the New York Times both ship. Reversed out of a solid square, it reads cleanly at 16 and stays unmistakably the same mark.

Candidate B

The tested T

What it explains. The T's stem turns at the foot and rises into a tick. One continuous stroke that reads as both a letter and a check mark — "what works, and how we'd know" compressed into a single gesture. The most literal of the three, and the easiest to explain in one sentence.

The risk. Tick marks are everywhere, and a tick can read as approval — as if the site endorses things. This publication's whole posture is that the answer is usually "it depends, and here is the evidence." If that bothers you, it is the wrong mark.

Candidate C

No mark at all

What it explains. The Economist, the Financial Times, the Guardian and the Atlantic do not have symbols. Their logo is the name, set in a face nobody else uses. For a publication, the wordmark carries more authority than any drawn mark, because it is the thing readers say out loud. Recognition comes from the letterforms and the setting.

The catch. You still need something square for a favicon, an app icon and a share-card corner. That would be TAP — the acronym anyone would use anyway, pronounceable, three letters, and unmistakable at any size.

Whichever you pick

It ships as a few hundred bytes of hand-written SVG with a single currentColor stroke, so it inherits the text colour, retints in dark mode for free, and never needs a second file. That replaces a 612 KB PNG on every page load.

Part two

Typefaces, taken from publishing rather than from software

Everything on this page is set in the real files, not a system stand-in — so what you are reading is the proposal itself. The monospace is gone, and so is everything else that reads as a developer tool rather than a publication.

Gone, and why

IBM Plex MonoProposed for the Instrument direction, and you are right that it is a tell. Plex was commissioned as a corporate technology typeface; on an editorial page a monospace says "terminal", not "publication". No monospace anywhere in the new stack — tabular figures come from the text faces instead, which is what newspapers have always done.
Inter · Space Grotesk · Geist · JetBrainsThe same family of tells. All excellent for interfaces, all instantly readable as the default choices of a generated site.
Plus Jakarta SansWhat the site uses today. A capable geometric sans that is now the house face of every startup landing page, and does no editorial work at all.
Playfair DisplayThe usual "make it look editorial" reflex. A high-contrast Didone that shatters at small sizes and appears on roughly half the sites reaching for gravitas.

Every one of them is the same recipe. A serif with real personality for headlines, a serif built for sustained reading in the body, and a grotesque — never a geometric sans, never a monospace — for labels, dates and figures. Not one of them uses a technology typeface anywhere. That is the pattern worth copying; the faces themselves are commissioned and unlicensable, so the job is to find the open equivalents.

Three display candidates

Real files · open licence · self-hosted

Newsreader — recommended

Production Type · OFL · variable, optical size

Can an Electric Car Catch a Criminal?

Drawn specifically for reading news on screen, with an optical-size axis: headlines get tighter and more contrasted while body text stays open and generous. It has a slight bookish warmth that keeps the register from turning cold, and its numerals are genuinely good — which matters on a site that prints figures constantly. The closest open face to something a broadsheet would commission.

Libre Baskerville

Impallari Type · OFL · the English transitional

Can an Electric Car Catch a Criminal?

Historically the most correct choice: Baskerville is the English transitional serif, and a Command Paper of any era would plausibly have been set in it. Screen-optimised here, with a large x-height. The cost is width — it takes up considerably more room than Newsreader at the same size, so headlines run longer and the two-column opening gets tight.

Literata

TypeTogether · OFL · variable

Can an Electric Car Catch a Criminal?

Commissioned for Google Play Books, so it is built for long-form reading above all else. Sturdier and more contemporary than the other two, with slab-ish serifs that hold up at small sizes. The most legible of the three and the least characterful — it would make an excellent body face under a Newsreader headline if you wanted the contrast.

The furniture face

Archivo

Omnibus-Type · OFL · variable weight + width

A nineteenth-century grotesque in the newspaper wood-type tradition — the same lineage as Franklin and Guardian Sans, which is exactly the furniture role in the table above. It carries a width axis, so condensed labels come from the same family rather than a second download, and its tabular figures line up properly in the register. Emphatically not a technology typeface.

Part three

Features worth stealing, and two worth deliberately not

Drawn from Police Oracle, the College of Policing and the way good news sites work. The site already has a lot — a crime dashboard, a perception explorer, a research assistant, practical summaries, audio editions, saved library, search. What follows is what is missing, ordered by what it would do for the reader against what it would cost you.

Build first · these define the site
01

An evidence-strength rating on every paper

The College of Policing rates every intervention in its Crime Reduction Toolkit against the EMMIE framework — effect, mechanism, moderators, implementation, economic cost — and shows the rating as a visible mark you can filter and sort on. This site's entire thesis is "how we'd know", and it currently asks readers to take the strength of each piece on trust. Rating the underlying evidence — systematic review, randomised trial, quasi-experimental, observational, single case, expert opinion — and printing it on the paper is the single most on-brand feature available, and it costs a frontmatter field plus a component.

Source · College of Policing, EMMIE
Small buildHigh value
02

A CPD log officers can actually export

Every officer and member of police staff has to evidence continuing professional development for their PDR, and almost nobody enjoys assembling it. The site already has accounts, a saved library and jsPDF. Add a button that records what was read, when, how many minutes and a free-text reflection, then exports the year as a signed-looking PDF. Police Oracle sells training; this gives away the paperwork, and it is the feature most likely to make someone create an account.

Source · Police Oracle training / CPD, and the PDR process itself
Small buildHighest pull
03

A "what the evidence says" index

The flagship. A browsable index of policing interventions — hot-spot patrol, focused deterrence, problem-oriented policing, body-worn video, stop and search, neighbourhood teams — each with the direction of effect, the strength of evidence behind it, a rough cost, and links to the papers here that cover it. This is the College's toolkit rewritten in this site's voice, which is worth doing because theirs is written for practitioners already inside the profession and yours is readable by anyone. Steal their "pin to compare" interaction while you are there.

04

A news register section, using the guide you already wrote

There is a full Police Oracle style guide sitting in the repository — inverted pyramid, present-perfect lead, attribution all the way down, a quarantined Analysis block — and the site publishes nothing in that register. The news on the homepage is aggregated third-party headlines that link away. Short reports of your own on HMICFRS publications, College releases, court outcomes and misconduct findings would give people a reason to come back weekly rather than when an essay lands, and the hardest part is already written.

05

Stat check, as a standing feature

"More or Less Policing" already exists as a topic. Make it a recurring, indexed feature with a fixed shape: the number in circulation, where it came from, what it actually measures, and a verdict. One a fortnight, short. It is the most shareable thing this site could publish, it feeds the news register, and it is the clearest possible demonstration of the masthead promise.

Source · BBC More or Less · Full Fact

06

A source index

You cite heavily. Nowhere can a reader see the whole body of evidence the site draws on. A single page listing every study, report and dataset cited anywhere — with which papers use it — is cheap to generate from existing citations, is genuinely useful to a researcher, and is a quiet, unarguable authority signal. Almost no site does this.

Source · academic practice · College research library

07

Corrections and methodology pages

The corrections block in the homepage mockup needs somewhere to live: a full log, dated, with what changed and whether the conclusion survived. Beside it, a page explaining how evidence is rated here and what the ratings mean. A publication that argues for evidence and does not publish its own error record is making an argument it has not accepted itself.

Reading paths by role

Police Oracle organises heavily around career stage — exams, promotion, talent pools. The equivalent here is not training, it is orientation: "new sergeant", "response officer", "analyst", "on a promotion board", "writing a business case" — each a short ordered path through papers that already exist. Pure curation over existing content, no new writing.

If you only do one

Do the evidence rating. It is a frontmatter field and a component, it changes nothing about how you write, and it turns the masthead promise into something a reader can see on every page rather than take on trust. Everything else on this list is easier to argue for once that exists — the what-works index is the rating applied to interventions instead of papers, and the CPD log is worth far more when what was read carries a strength.
